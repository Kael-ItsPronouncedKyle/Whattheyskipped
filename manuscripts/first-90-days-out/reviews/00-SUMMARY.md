## What every reader agreed on

- The book is careful, kind, and honest about its sources. All seven said they would trust it more than most of what's out there, and several said they would hand it to someone.
- **Keep:** the crisis page as the very first page; the practical-safety chapter; the honest caveats on the religious-trauma research; the charitable reading of frightened parents; the warning that high-control habits show up in any group, religious or not; the FAQ line about coming back "on purpose."
- The problems are mostly **about what's missing, not what's said.** Almost every fix is an addition, not a rewrite.

## The five themes behind the 34 must-fix notes

### 1. Trans and nonbinary readers disappear after the author's note (trans woman, trans man, nonbinary: 16 must-fix)
- The words "nonbinary" and "transgender" never appear in the body of the book.
- **Purity culture (Ch. 8)** is written as "women got this, men got that." Trans readers were raised on the other gender's script and then handed the "wrong" sentence.
- **Complementarianism** ("biblical manhood and womanhood") gets one glossary line, though for many readers it shaped childhood more than hell did.
- **Family scripts** assume a mom-and-dad household and treat being deadnamed as a "disagreement." The kid script "Mom and I…" assumes the reader is a father.
- **Practical safety (Ch. 3)** leaves out documents (birth certificate, Social Security card, passport), hormones and prescriptions, parent-held insurance statements and patient portals, and physical safety at church events.
- **Conversion therapy (Ch. 13)** names the rebrands aimed at sexual orientation but not those aimed at gender identity. The therapist questions don't ask about trans clients or about letters for gender-affirming care.
- **Crisis fallback:** after hours, when Trans Lifeline is closed, every option for someone over 25 can involve emergency services. The book says so only about 988.

### 2. The neutrality promise isn't fully kept (conservative layperson, conservative pastor, progressive pastor: 12 must-fix)
- **Who counts as safe:** every "safe" example in the Ch. 5 safety map is non-religious or progressive, and every "not yet" example is devout.
- **"High-control" is never defined,** so it slides into meaning "conservative."
- **Questions of fact vs. thinking errors:** Ch. 10 makes real yes-or-no questions (did Jesus rise?) sound like all-or-nothing thinking.
- **Ethics:** Ch. 11 settles a contested question ("at best, they gave them a vocabulary") without naming the argument that morality depends on God.
- **Where people land:** Ch. 14 has no portrait for staying in, or returning to, historic or evangelical faith.
- **Part Three assumes the reader has left** both church and God (both pastors flagged this).
- **Further reading:** there are no orthodox titles, and no progressive Christian ones beyond McLaren.
- **Purity culture:** Ch. 8 offers no path for someone who rejects the shame but keeps the traditional sexual ethic.
- **The pattern underneath:** the book describes conservative teaching through its worst practices, without saying that conservative theology itself rejects many of them (for example, treating doubt as sin, or blaming girls for boys' lust).

### 3. The book isn't clear enough about harm (progressive layperson: 6 must-fix)
- It should say once, plainly: *this book is neutral about where you land, not about abuse.*
- **Abuse gets a single bullet point.** There is no section on clergy abuse or cover-ups, and nothing saying forgiveness can't be demanded of you.
- **No crisis lines for abuse survivors:** add hotlines for domestic violence, sexual assault, child abuse, and Native survivors.
- **Politics and Christian nationalism are absent,** even though they are why many readers left.
- **Race is never mentioned.**
- **The "both" exercise (Ch. 10) and "keep the person" (Ch. 6)** need a carve-out, so survivors don't read them as a demand to soften abuse.

### 4. Accuracy about conservative institutions (conservative pastor: 2 must-fix)
- **Biblical counseling** is described in a way its own movement wouldn't recognize, and the cited source doesn't support the wording.
- **Church membership and formal church discipline** are missing from Practical Safety. For members of conservative churches, they are the most predictable consequence of leaving.

### 5. Staying Christian is treated as a footnote (progressive pastor: 3 must-fix)
- **Affirming status:** Ch. 14 lists Catholicism, Orthodoxy, and progressive evangelical churches next to affirming mainline churches, with no warning to LGBTQ+ readers that the first three are not officially affirming.
- **Other kinds of help:** Ch. 13 never mentions spiritual directors or affirming clergy.

## Where readers pull in different directions

| Tension | Readers on each side | How to fix both |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral vs. clear about harm | Conservative readers want more balance; the progressive reader wants a clear stance on harm | These don't actually conflict. Say outright that the book is neutral about **belief** and not neutral about **abuse**, and define "high-control" by behaviors, not doctrine. |
| Politics and Christian nationalism | The progressive reader wants a section; a conservative reader would likely stop trusting the book | Write it descriptively: "for many readers, the 2016–2020 years were the breaking point," sourced and without editorializing. Or leave it out. **Your call.** |
| Orthodox titles in Further Reading | Conservatives want them; progressive readers may see it as recruiting | Use clearly labeled subsections: "If you want the strongest case for staying," "If you want a different kind of Christianity," "If you're leaving." |
| Length | Every reader asks for additions | Most fixes are one to three paragraphs. Together they would add roughly 15–20 pages, taking the book to about 115–120 pages, close to Unbound's 127. |
