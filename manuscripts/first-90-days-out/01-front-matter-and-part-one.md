## BEFORE YOU BEGIN

### A NOTE ON WHERE YOU ARE

You might have walked out of a church on purpose, with a speech prepared. You might have been walked out: asked to step down from the worship team, quietly dropped from the small group text, told by a parent that you were welcome home as long as you didn't talk about it. You might still be going every Sunday, singing words you're no longer sure you mean, and reading this on your phone in the parking lot afterward.

All three of those people are welcome here. So is the person who hasn't left anything yet and is only checking what's on the other side of the door.

This workbook doesn't care where you end up. Some people who go through a season like this come out with a different faith. Some come out with none. Some come out with something they don't have a word for yet. None of those is the correct answer, and none of them is the goal of this book. The goal is smaller and more urgent: that you get through the next ninety days with your health, your relationships, your money, and your sense of yourself as intact as possible, and that you do the deciding later, on purpose, when the ground has stopped moving.

### WHAT THIS WORKBOOK IS NOT

It is not therapy. A workbook can do a lot. It can't notice that you've stopped eating, or hear the thing you didn't write down. Chapter 13 covers how to find a therapist who won't make things worse.

It is not a deconversion manual. There is no step where you're supposed to stop believing in God, and no bonus page for reaching atheism by Day 60.

It is not a manual for staying, either. If you came hoping someone would talk you back into certainty, this isn't that book. There are many of those, and you have probably read some of them already.

It is not anti-Christian. A lot of what follows was written with gratitude for the good things many people found in church: the music, the casseroles, the people who showed up at the hospital. Leaving a system doesn't require pretending nothing in it was real.

### HOW THE NINETY DAYS WORK

The book has three parts, one for each month.

**Part One, Days 1–30: Steady the Ground.** Safety, sleep, money, fear, and who to tell. Nothing gets decided in Part One. If you decide anything big in the first month, you're allowed to, but this book won't push you to.

**Part Two, Days 31–60: Look at What You Were Given.** Once your feet are under you, you sort through what you were taught, and grieve what you've lost. That includes the things you're glad to lose, which can still be grieved.

**Part Three, Days 61–90: Build Something on Purpose.** Ethics, community, help, and where people land. You start choosing instead of reacting.

Each part has a few short chapters and thirty workbook pages. One page a day. Most take five to ten minutes.

### THREE WAYS THROUGH

**Daily.** One page a day, in order. This is the version the book was designed for.

**Weekly.** Read one chapter on a weekend morning and do the week's pages in one sitting. Slower, and fine.

**Crisis only.** You don't do the pages at all. You keep the book nearby and open it to the chapter that matches the worst thing happening this week. The contents page is your index.

You can skip days. You can quit for a month and come back. There is no streak to break. Many people who grew up evangelical spent years measuring their spiritual lives in unbroken quiet times and guilty gaps. You don't need a new version of that.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

If you have the PDF, print the workbook pages, or keep a plain notebook next to you and write the date at the top of each page. If you're worried someone will read it, use a notebook you can keep with you, or a notes app with a passcode. Privacy is a reasonable thing to want right now, and Chapter 3 talks about it.

### A NOTE ON WHO WROTE THIS

This workbook was written by a trans man who spent more than ten years leading Bible studies. That means he has been on both sides of the folding table: the one passing out the handouts, and later the one the handouts were about.

By training he is an epidemiologist, and he spent fifteen years building supply chain systems. Neither job is glamorous, but both teach the same habit: when something goes wrong, you don't blame the person standing closest to the problem. You trace the chain. Where did this come from? Who handled it? What was it exposed to on the way? This workbook applies that habit to a different kind of problem: a person whose whole world was built on one set of answers, standing in the middle of the floor after it shifted.

He went through his own version of these ninety days. He took apart the theology he was handed and rebuilt from the studs. Some walls didn't pass inspection. Some did. He mentions that only so you know where he's standing, not to suggest you should end up standing there too. If you finish this book somewhere completely different from where he landed, and you got there honestly and in one piece, the book did its job.

He is not a therapist, and this book doesn't pretend otherwise. What it offers is what he wished someone had handed him early on: practical steps, honest context with the sources named, and permission to take this one page at a time.

## 01 · WHAT JUST HAPPENED

*You don't need the right word for it. But the words can help.*

### 1.1 THE WORDS PEOPLE USE

People going through this use a handful of words, often interchangeably, and it helps to know what each one usually means.

**Deconstruction.** The word comes from twentieth-century philosophy, mostly associated with the French philosopher Jacques Derrida (who adapted it from Martin Heidegger). There it named a way of reading texts that exposes the oppositions and assumptions they depend on. In church conversations in recent years it has come to mean something looser: taking apart what you were taught, piece by piece, to see what holds. Deconstruction doesn't have a fixed destination. Plenty of people deconstruct and remain Christian.

**Deconversion.** Leaving a faith altogether. The word is used by sociologists of religion as well as by people describing themselves.

**Faith transition** or **faith shift.** Gentler umbrella terms. Some therapists and support groups prefer them because they don't assume an outcome.

**Exvangelical.** A person who grew up in, or spent years inside, evangelical Christianity and has left it, at least in its evangelical form. Blake Chastain coined the term in 2016 as a hashtag and the name of his podcast, and it spread quickly online.

**Church hurt.** Usually means harm done by a particular church or leader, often used by people who still consider themselves Christian.

**Religious trauma.** Covered in Chapter 2. It describes an effect on a person, not a position on God.

You can use any of these, all of them, or none. Some people find that having a word makes them feel less alone. Others find that every label sounds like joining a new team, and they've had enough of teams for a while. Both responses are reasonable.

### 1.2 WHY IT FEELS SO BIG

If you grew up in a high-commitment church, your faith wasn't one part of your life. It was the operating system. It decided:

- who your friends were, and whose house you could sleep over at
- what you did on Sunday mornings, Wednesday nights, and every summer
- what music you listened to and what movies you weren't allowed to see
- who you could date, and what you could do on the date
- how you understood your own body, your sadness, and your future
- what happens after you die, and to everyone you love

When the operating system starts failing, everything running on it stutters at once. That's why something that looks from the outside like "changing your mind about some theology" can feel from the inside like a house fire. You aren't overreacting. You're reacting to the size of the thing.

### 1.3 HOW IT USUALLY STARTS

There's no single path, but people describe a few common starting points:

- **A question that wouldn't go away.** Hell, the age of the earth, the "clobber passages," why prayer seemed to work for the youth pastor's parking spots and not for your mother's cancer.
- **A person.** A gay friend, a sibling who came out, a coworker from another religion who was obviously kinder than the elders.
- **A betrayal.** An abusive leader protected by the church. A cover-up. A financial scandal.
- **Your own body.** Coming out, a divorce, an illness, depression that didn't lift when you prayed harder.
- **Education.** A college class, a podcast, a book. Sometimes the thing that tipped it was learning a fact the church left out. (That's why this website is called what it's called.)

None of these starting points makes your conclusions right or wrong. They just make it clear that you didn't wake up one morning and decide to be difficult.

### 1.4 WHAT YOU DON'T HAVE TO KNOW YET

- You don't have to know whether God exists.
- You don't have to know what you'll tell your parents.
- You don't have to know whether you'll ever go back to church, or a church, or any building with a steeple.
- You don't have to know what happens when you die.

For the next thirty days, the only job is to keep yourself steady enough to think about those questions later. If you want to think about them now, go ahead. But you're allowed to say, "Not this month."

## 02 · YOUR BODY IS PART OF THIS

*Fear lives in the nervous system, not only in your beliefs.*

### 2.1 WHAT PEOPLE NOTICE

People in the first months after leaving a high-control faith often describe symptoms that seem out of proportion to "a change of opinion." The most common ones:

- trouble sleeping, especially lying awake afraid of hell, or of the Rapture having happened while they slept
- a jolt of panic when they hear a worship song in a store
- intrusive "what if I'm wrong" thoughts that loop for hours
- guilt that arrives with no particular thing to be guilty about
- feeling numb, flat, or far away from their own life
- anger that seems too big for the moment it shows up in
- anxiety around sex, bodies, or pleasure, even when they no longer believe the rules they were taught

If you recognize yourself in that list, you're not weak and you're not broken. You spent years in a system that taught your body to treat certain thoughts as dangerous. The beliefs can change in an afternoon. The alarm system takes longer to update.

### 2.2 "RELIGIOUS TRAUMA": WHAT THE TERM MEANS AND WHAT IT DOESN'T

In 2011 the psychologist Marlene Winell published a series of articles in *CBT Today*, the members' magazine of the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies, describing what she called **Religious Trauma Syndrome**. After years of working with people leaving authoritarian religious groups, she had noticed a pattern of symptoms that looked a lot like the effects of other kinds of trauma.

Three things are worth knowing about the term.

**It is not a formal diagnosis.** Religious Trauma Syndrome isn't in the DSM-5-TR, the manual American clinicians use to diagnose mental health conditions. The closest the manual comes is a code for a "religious or spiritual problem," which marks something as a focus of treatment rather than diagnosing a disorder. What clinicians can diagnose and treat are the conditions that sometimes come with religious trauma, such as anxiety disorders, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder.

**It describes effects, not theology.** Saying someone has religious trauma doesn't mean every religion is traumatic, or that everyone who grew up in your church was harmed. Some of your old friends had a very different experience in the same pews. Both experiences can be true at once.

**The research is young.** A 2023 survey study by Darren Slade and colleagues estimated that roughly 27 to 33 percent of American adults, around one in three, have experienced religious trauma at some point in their lives. That's a striking number, and it's also an early one: the researchers themselves note that the term is still defined loosely across the field, the study was funded, carried out, and published by the research center its authors belong to, and other researchers have questioned how its sample was drawn. Treat the number as a sign that you're far from alone, not as a settled fact.

### 2.3 WHAT HELPS IN THE SHORT TERM

None of this replaces professional help. These are the basic steadying tools that therapists commonly recommend for anxiety, adapted for this particular kind.

- **Name it when it happens.** "This is the hell fear again." Labeling a feeling doesn't make it disappear, but it reminds the thinking part of your brain that it's a familiar alarm, not new information.
- **Go physical before you go theological.** When you're spiraling at 2 a.m., don't try to win the argument about eternal punishment. Get up, drink water, put your feet on the floor, breathe out longer than you breathe in. Argue with the theology in daylight.
- **Limit the late-night research.** It's tempting to read apologetics and counter-apologetics until 3 a.m. Set a cutoff time.
- **Keep the basics.** Sleep, food, daylight, some movement. Boring, and still the foundation.
- **Tell one safe person.** More on this in Chapter 5.

### 2.4 WHEN TO GET MORE HELP

Get help from a professional, soon, if any of these is true for more than a couple of weeks:

- you can't sleep, or can't get out of bed
- you've stopped eating, or can't stop
- you're drinking or using more than usual to get through the evenings
- panic attacks are happening several times a week
- you've thought about hurting yourself, or about not being here

If the last one is true today, go back to the first page of this book.

3. Write one sentence you can say to yourself when it does. For example: "This is an old alarm. I'm safe in this room."

## 03 · PRACTICAL SAFETY

*Before the big questions: a roof, money, and a phone nobody else controls.*

This is the least philosophical chapter in the book, and for some readers it's the most important. Changing your beliefs can cost you things in the real world: a job at a Christian school, housing that came through the church, a parent's financial support, sometimes a marriage. Before you make any announcements, look at what you depend on.

### 3.1 THE DEPENDENCY CHECKLIST

Go through this list and mark anything that comes through your church, your family's approval, or a religious institution.

- **Housing.** Do you live with family, in church-owned housing, or with roommates from church?
- **Income.** Do you work for a church, ministry, Christian school, or a business owned by church members?
- **Health insurance.** Is it through a religious employer or a parent's plan?
- **School.** Are you enrolled at a religious college with a faith statement or conduct code? Could a change in belief, or coming out, affect your enrollment or financial aid?
- **Phone and accounts.** Who pays for your phone plan? Who can see your location, messages, and email?
- **Transportation.** Whose name is the car in?
- **Childcare and kids.** Who watches your children? If you share custody, does a parenting agreement mention religious upbringing?

Anything you marked is something you may want to plan around before you have big conversations. Not because you should hide forever, but because it's easier to be honest when your rent isn't riding on someone's reaction.

### 3.2 IF YOU'RE LIVING WITH FAMILY

If you're a teenager or young adult living with religious parents, you're in the hardest spot in this book, and some of the advice for adults doesn't apply to you. A few things that do:

- **You don't owe anyone a full account of your beliefs before you're ready.** That's different from lying about other things. It's the same discretion everyone uses about private matters.
- **Find one adult outside the house who's safe.** A teacher, a school counselor, a relative, a friend's parent.
- **If you're LGBTQ+ and worried about being kicked out,** the Trevor Project (listed on the first page of this book) offers support and can help you think through options. Many cities have organizations that help young people who've been forced out of their homes.
- **Count down, if you can.** Many people in your situation decide to wait until they've finished school or moved out before having the big conversation. That isn't cowardice. It's planning.

### 3.3 PRIVACY

If someone in your house checks your phone or browser history:

- use a private browsing window for research
- keep this workbook somewhere you control, or use a passcode-protected notes app
- think before joining public online groups under your real name

Privacy isn't the same as shame. You're allowed to think in private before you speak in public.

### 3.4 IF YOU WORK FOR A RELIGIOUS EMPLOYER

In the US, religious organizations have broad legal room to require employees to share their beliefs, and some employment-law protections that apply to other workplaces don't apply to them in the same way. If your paycheck depends on your faith statement:

- quietly read your contract and the employee handbook
- start saving, if you can
- update your résumé so that it reads well outside church settings ("coordinated events for 300 attendees" rather than "ran youth group lock-ins")
- talk to an employment lawyer before you resign or disclose anything, if your situation is complicated

The exemptions are broadest for churches, ministries, and religious schools. An ordinary business that happens to be owned by Christians generally doesn't get them. This book can't give legal advice, and the law varies by state and country. Please treat this section as a list of questions to ask, not answers.

1. List everything you marked on the dependency checklist.
2. For each one, write one small step that would make you less dependent on it in the next six months.
3. Who is one adult outside your church and family that you could call if things went badly?

## 04 · THE HELL QUESTION, FIRST-AID VERSION

*You can't settle the doctrine tonight. You can take the panic down a notch.*

For a lot of people, the hardest part of the first month isn't losing community or certainty. It's the fear: *What if they were right? What if I'm going to hell? What if my little brother is?*

This chapter is first aid, not the full treatment. The longer study belongs in daylight, and the What They Skipped guides cover it in depth. Here's what you need to know for now.

### 4.1 WHY THE FEAR STICKS

If you were taught about hell as a child, you heard about it before you could evaluate it. Children don't weigh evidence about eternal punishment. They absorb it. That fear got stored early, deep, and alongside your love for the people who taught it to you. That's why adults who no longer believe in hell sometimes still wake up afraid of it. The belief left; the fear stayed.

### 4.2 FOUR FACTS THAT HELP

These don't settle the question. They loosen the grip.

1. **Christians have never been unanimous about hell.** Eternal conscious punishment has been the majority view for most of church history, but serious Christian thinkers have also held annihilation (the wicked cease to exist) and universal reconciliation (everyone is eventually restored). All three have published defenders today. Eternal punishment is still the official position of most large denominations; annihilation is a recognized minority view among evangelicals; universal reconciliation has prominent defenders but is officially rejected by the largest churches.
2. **The English word "hell" translates several different words.** The Hebrew Bible's *Sheol* is the realm of the dead, where everyone goes, good or bad. The New Testament uses *Hades*, *Gehenna* (named after the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem), and once a verb form of *Tartarus* (2 Peter 2:4). The King James Version used the single English word "hell" for Gehenna, Hades, the Tartarus verse, and about half the occurrences of Sheol. That blurred distinctions the original languages keep, and later translations such as the American Standard Version (1901) restored some of them.
3. **Fear is not evidence.** How afraid you feel tells you about your training, not about the afterlife.
4. **Many people who believe in God don't believe in hell.** In a 2021 Pew Research Center survey, 91 percent of American adults said they believe in God or a higher power, but only 62 percent said they believe in hell. You don't have to resolve the whole God question to set this particular fear down.

### 4.3 WHAT TO DO AT 2 A.M.

- Go back to section 2.3 and do the physical steps first.
- Say the sentence you wrote at the end of Chapter 2.
- Write the fear down in one sentence, close the notebook, and tell yourself you'll look at it in daylight. Then look at it in daylight, so that your brain learns the promise is real.

## 05 · WHO TO TELL, AND WHEN

*Rank people by safety, not by closeness.*

### 5.1 YOU DON'T HAVE TO TELL ANYONE YET

There's a strong pull, especially for people raised in churches that valued testimony, to announce what's happening. Some of that pull is healthy: secrets are heavy. Some of it is the old habit of thinking a change doesn't count until it's public.

You can tell everyone this week. You can tell one person. You can tell no one for a year. All three are honest choices. The question isn't "Am I brave enough to tell?" It's "What do I need, and who can safely give it to me?"

### 5.2 THE SAFETY MAP

The people closest to you aren't always the safest people to tell first. Your mom may love you more than anyone alive and still be the person most likely to panic, cry, call your youth pastor, and pray for you loudly in the family group chat.

Sort the people in your life into four groups:

| Group | Who goes here | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Safe now** | Will listen without trying to fix you or win you back | A friend who left church years ago; a non-religious coworker |
| **Safe later** | Loves you, will probably come around, needs time | A sibling; a progressive aunt |
| **Not yet** | Likely to react with fear, pressure, or punishment | Parents in leadership; a spouse still deeply invested |
| **Maybe never** | Telling them would cost more than it gives | A boss at a Christian school; an abusive ex |

People move between groups over time. This is a map for this month, not a final ruling.

### 5.3 WHAT TO SAY TO THE FIRST PERSON

You don't need a speech. Something like:

> "I've been going through some stuff with my faith. I'm not looking for answers right now. I just didn't want to carry it by myself. Can I talk to you about it sometimes?"

That asks for what you need, which is company, not debate. It also tells the other person what their job is.

### 5.4 WHEN SOMEONE REACTS BADLY

If someone responds with panic, anger, or a stack of apologetics, you can end the conversation without winning it:

> "I can tell this is hard for you to hear. I'm not going to argue about it today. I'll come back to it when we're both calmer."

Part Two has longer scripts for parents and spouses, and Appendix C has short ones for pastors and others.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Fill in the safety map in 5.2 with real names, or initials if you want privacy.
2. Who is one person in "Safe now"? When could you talk to them this week?
3. Write the first sentence you'd say to them, in your own words.

## WORKBOOK · DAYS 1–30

One prompt per day. Write the date at the top of each page. If you skip a day, skip it; don't double up.

**Week 1: Orientation**

- **Day 1.** The snapshot. Write down, as honestly as you can, what you believe today about God, the Bible, Jesus, the afterlife, and church. Date it. You'll look at this again on Day 90.
- **Day 2.** What's one thing you're afraid will happen in the next month? What's one thing you hope will happen?
- **Day 3.** Describe your Sunday mornings as a kid in as much sensory detail as you can: smells, sounds, what you wore.
- **Day 4.** What did church give you that you'd miss? Make a list, even if it's short.
- **Day 5.** Rate your sleep this week from 1 to 10. What helps? What makes it worse?
- **Day 6.** Write about one person who was kind to you in church.
- **Day 7.** Look back at the week. What was harder than you expected? What was easier?

**Week 2: The Body**

- **Day 8.** Where in your body do you feel fear? Chest, stomach, throat, somewhere else?
- **Day 9.** Do the breathing exercise from section 2.3 for two minutes. Write what you noticed.
- **Day 10.** Write down one song, place, or phrase that sets off an old alarm. What happens when you encounter it?
- **Day 11.** What's one thing you could do for your body today that has nothing to do with belief? Do it. Write about it.
- **Day 12.** What were you taught about anger? Is that still true for you?
- **Day 13.** When did you last feel genuinely at peace? Where were you?
- **Day 14.** Look back at the week. Any pattern in when the fear shows up?

**Week 3: Ground Under Your Feet**

- **Day 15.** Finish the dependency checklist from Chapter 3 if you haven't.
- **Day 16.** Choose one small step from your Chapter 3 list. What would it take to do it this month?
- **Day 17.** What's your money situation, honestly? What would you need to feel less trapped?
- **Day 18.** If you had to leave your current living situation tomorrow, where could you go?
- **Day 19.** What do you want to keep private for now? From whom?
- **Day 20.** Write about something you're good at that has nothing to do with church.
- **Day 21.** Look back at the week. What feels a little more solid than it did on Day 1?

**Week 4: The Fear and the People**

- **Day 22.** Write your hell fear in one sentence. Then write the date you'll look at it in daylight.
- **Day 23.** Look at it in daylight. Is it the same size as last night?
- **Day 24.** Fill in your safety map from Chapter 5.
- **Day 25.** Draft what you'd say to one "Safe now" person.
- **Day 26.** If you told that person, how did it go? If you didn't, what stopped you?
- **Day 27.** Who do you miss? Write them a letter you won't send.
- **Day 28.** What would you tell a friend in your exact situation?
- **Day 29.** What do you need most going into the next month?
- **Day 30.** Re-read your Day 1 snapshot. Don't change it. Write underneath: what's one thing that has shifted, even slightly?
