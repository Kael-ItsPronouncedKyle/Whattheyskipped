# PART TWO · DAYS 31–60

## LOOK AT WHAT YOU WERE GIVEN

You've made it a month. That's worth saying out loud, because the first month is usually the loudest. If the ground still feels like it's moving, that's normal. If it has gone strangely quiet, that's normal too.

Part Two is where you start looking at what you were handed: the beliefs, habits, and rules you absorbed before you could choose them. You'll sort them, grieve some of them, and keep some of them. You're not trying to finish anything this month. You're trying to see clearly.

---

## 06 · SORTING THE INHERITANCE

*Not everything you were given was a lie. Not everything was a gift.*

### 6.1 THE PROBLEM WITH ALL OR NOTHING

High-control churches often teach faith as a package deal. If you doubt the creation account, you've doubted Jesus. If you question one doctrine, the whole thing collapses. Some churches say this out loud ("If Genesis isn't literal, why trust any of it?"). Others teach it by how they react when someone asks a question.

The trouble is that people who leave often carry the same package-deal logic out the door with them. *If they were wrong about hell, they were wrong about everything. If the youth pastor lied, then kindness and generosity and singing together were fake too.*

They weren't. The package was a teaching method, not a fact about the world. You're allowed to open it and look at each item on its own.

### 6.2 THREE PILES

Take anything you were taught and put it in one of three piles.

**Keep.** True, good, or useful, whatever its source. Examples people often put here: generosity, showing up for the sick, honesty, a love of old hymns, the habit of reading carefully, the idea that every person has worth.

**Return.** Harmful, false, or not yours. Examples people often put here: the belief that your body is dangerous, fear of hell as a motivator, the idea that doubt is sin, rules about gender that never fit you, the instinct to report your own thoughts to an authority.

**Unexamined.** You don't know yet. This pile is allowed to be the biggest one for a long time. Examples: what you think about prayer, whether the Bible is special, what you think happens after death.

Most people are surprised by two things when they do this. First, the "keep" pile is longer than they expected. Second, things they thought they'd rejected keep turning up in their reactions, which means they belong in "unexamined," not "return."

### 6.3 SEPARATING THE TEACHING FROM THE TEACHER

Sometimes a teaching is tangled up with a person. You loved your grandmother, and she taught you that rock music was demonic. You admired a youth leader, and he taught you that your sexuality was a test.

You can keep the person and return the teaching. Your grandmother was not her worst idea. Neither are you.

The reverse is also true. Sometimes good teaching came from someone who hurt you. You can keep the teaching without excusing the person.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Make the three piles. Write at least five items in each, if you can.
2. Pick one item from "keep." Where did you learn it, and from whom?
3. Pick one item from "return." What did it cost you to carry it?

## 07 · GRIEF WITHOUT A FUNERAL

*Some losses don't come with casseroles.*

### 7.1 WHAT PEOPLE LOSE

When people leave a faith community, they often lose things nobody names as losses:

- the friends who stopped texting
- the weekly rhythm of Sunday, and the feeling of belonging somewhere at a set time
- singing with a room full of people
- certainty about what happens to people you love who've died
- the future you'd planned: the church wedding, raising kids in the same youth group, the ministry career
- your place in your family, or the version of you your family loved
- sometimes, God, or the sense of being accompanied

And the strange one: you can grieve things you're glad to be rid of. Someone who escaped a controlling marriage can still grieve the marriage they hoped for. You can grieve a church that hurt you.

### 7.2 WHAT GRIEF RESEARCH ACTUALLY SAYS

You've probably heard of the "five stages of grief": denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. They come from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's 1969 book *On Death and Dying*, which was based on interviews with people facing their own deaths, not people grieving someone else, and certainly not people leaving a religion. Kübler-Ross herself later said the stages were never meant to be a tidy sequence.

Two later ideas from grief research fit this situation better.

**Ambiguous loss.** The family therapist Pauline Boss coined this term for losses with no clear ending: a loved one who is physically present but psychologically gone, or physically gone with no confirmation of death. Leaving a faith often looks like this. Your parents are still alive, but the relationship you had with them is gone. Your church is still there, two miles away, but you can't go back. Boss's point is that ambiguous losses are harder to grieve because there's no ritual and no moment where everyone agrees something has ended.

**The dual process model.** Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut described healthy grieving as moving back and forth between two modes: facing the loss directly (crying, remembering, writing the letter) and turning away from it to rebuild ordinary life (going to work, learning something new, laughing at a dumb show). Moving back and forth isn't avoidance. It's how grief works.

### 7.3 MAKING YOUR OWN RITUAL

Since nobody is going to hold a funeral for your old faith, you may want to make a small ritual of your own. Some people:

- write a letter to the God they were taught about, and keep it or burn it
- go to one last service on purpose, sit in the back, and leave before the end
- box up the old study Bibles and journals and put them in a closet rather than throwing them away
- plant something
- sing one old song alone, all the way through

There's no correct ritual. The point is to mark the ending yourself instead of waiting for someone else to.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. List everything you've lost, including the things you're glad to be rid of.
2. Which loss on your list feels "ambiguous," in Boss's sense?
3. Plan a small ritual. When will you do it?

## 08 · PURITY CULTURE AND YOUR BODY

*You were taught to be afraid of the body you live in.*

### 8.1 WHAT "PURITY CULTURE" MEANS

"Purity culture" is the name people now use for a movement in American evangelicalism that peaked in the 1990s and 2000s. It included:

- abstinence pledges and purity rings, often through programs like True Love Waits, which the Southern Baptist Convention launched in 1993
- books like Joshua Harris's *I Kissed Dating Goodbye* (1997), which told teenagers to avoid dating altogether
- the idea that sexual purity was a gift you'd give your future spouse, and that losing it made you "used"
- modesty teaching that held girls responsible for boys' thoughts
- the promise, sometimes explicit, that waiting would be rewarded with a great marriage and great sex

If you grew up in a different decade or a different branch of the church, you may recognize some of this and not the rest.

In 2018, Harris publicly said he no longer agreed with *I Kissed Dating Goodbye*, and its publisher stopped printing it at his request. A book that shaped a generation was withdrawn by its own author. It's worth knowing that, if you were raised on it.

### 8.2 WHAT PEOPLE CARRY OUT OF IT

The journalist Linda Kay Klein spent twelve years interviewing women raised in purity culture for her book *Pure* (2018). Many of them described:

- shame and anxiety around sex that didn't go away after marriage
- feeling disconnected from their own bodies
- fear that their desires made them dangerous
- difficulty knowing what they wanted, because they'd been trained to suppress the question

A large survey of evangelical women by Sheila Wray Gregoire and her co-authors, published as *The Great Sex Rescue* (2021), found that women who had believed certain purity teachings as teenagers reported more sexual pain and less satisfaction in marriage. Gregoire writes from inside evangelicalism, which makes the findings harder for her own readers to wave away.

Purity culture didn't only affect women. Men describe being taught that they were one glance away from lust at all times, and that their bodies were enemies to be managed. LGBTQ+ people were often taught that their bodies were a problem with no solution.

### 8.3 WHAT HELPS

- **Unlearning takes longer than learning.** You absorbed this over years. It won't leave in a weekend.
- **Your body isn't a moral test.** Hunger, desire, rest, and pleasure are normal parts of being an animal with a nervous system.
- **Talk to the right professional.** If you're experiencing sexual pain or anxiety, a medical doctor and a sex-positive therapist can help. Pelvic floor physical therapy is a real specialty. You're allowed to ask a provider whether they have experience with clients from conservative religious backgrounds.
- **Your timeline is yours.** Some people react to purity culture by doing everything they were forbidden, all at once. Some people want to go slowly. Neither is the right way to heal. Safety and consent still matter, for you as much as anyone.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. What were you taught about your body, and who taught it?
2. Which of those teachings do you still feel in your body, even if your mind has moved on?
3. Write one sentence about your body that you'd like to believe a year from now.

## 09 · FAMILY, MARRIAGE, AND MIXED-FAITH HOMES

*You can love people who think you're lost.*

### 9.1 WHY FAMILY IS THE HARDEST PART

For many people, the hardest thing about leaving isn't the theology. It's watching someone they love become afraid for them. If your parents believe in eternal punishment, then from their point of view you're in danger. Their panic might feel like rejection. Sometimes it is rejection. Often it's fear wearing the costume of anger.

Understanding that doesn't mean you have to accept bad treatment. It just helps you decide what to say.

### 9.2 PARENTS

What parents often say, and what they often mean:

| They say | They often mean |
|---|---|
| "Where did we go wrong?" | "I'm scared this is my fault." |
| "You're just going through a phase." | "I need this to be temporary." |
| "Who has been influencing you?" | "I can't believe you got here on your own." |
| "We'll be praying for you." | Sometimes love. Sometimes a way to end the conversation. Often both. |

A few things you can say:

> "I'm not doing this to hurt you. I'm doing it because I have to be honest about what I think."

> "I know you're scared for me. I'm okay. I'd like us to keep having dinner together even though we disagree."

> "I'm not going to debate this with you. If you want to understand where I am, I'm happy to tell you. If you want to change my mind, I'm going to leave."

### 9.3 SPOUSES AND PARTNERS

If you're married to someone who still believes, you may be facing the hardest version of this. Many couples who married inside the church made promises with a shared faith at the center. When one person changes, both people can feel betrayed.

Some mixed-faith marriages last and some don't. Things couples in that position often find helpful:

- talking about what each person is afraid of, not only what each person believes
- agreeing on what religious practices the household will keep, and which are individual
- not making the other person's faith or doubt the subject of every argument
- seeing a couples therapist who isn't trying to win either of you over

If your spouse responds with control, threats, or cutting you off from money or friends, that's not a theology problem. It's a safety problem. Go back to Chapter 3.

### 9.4 KIDS

If you have children, you may be asking what to tell them and whether to keep taking them to church. There's no single answer. Some principles that many parents in your position use:

- Be honest at their level: "Mom and Dad believe different things about God, and that's okay."
- Don't use your kids as messengers between you and their other parent, or their grandparents.
- Let them ask questions. It's fine to say, "I don't know. What do you think?"
- Protect them from fear-based teaching, whichever direction it comes from.

### 9.5 HOLIDAYS

Christmas and Easter can be loaded. You might choose to go to the service with family, skip it, or go to dinner but not church. Decide ahead of time what you'll do, tell the people who need to know, and have an exit plan if things get tense.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Which family member is hardest for you right now? What do you think they're afraid of?
2. Pick one script from 9.2 and rewrite it in your own words.
3. Plan the next holiday. What will you do, and what's your exit plan?

## 10 · YOUR BRAIN ON BLACK-AND-WHITE THINKING

*You can leave the system and still think the way it trained you to.*

### 10.1 THE PATTERN

Many high-control churches teach in absolutes. Saved or lost. Heaven or hell. Truth or lies. God's way or the world's way. If you spent years thinking in those categories, your brain learned to sort everything into two bins.

When people leave, they often keep the bins and just swap the labels. The church used to be all good and now it's all bad. Their parents used to be heroes and now they're villains. They used to be sure God was real, and now they're sure, with the same intensity, that anyone who believes is a fool.

This is common, and it usually softens with time. But it helps to notice it.

### 10.2 WHAT PSYCHOLOGY CALLS IT

Cognitive therapists call this pattern **all-or-nothing thinking** (sometimes "black-and-white" or "dichotomous" thinking). It appears in the lists of cognitive distortions developed by Aaron Beck and made popular by David Burns in *Feeling Good* (1980). It isn't unique to religion. People use it in diets, relationships, and politics. Religion that teaches in absolutes just gives it a lot of practice.

### 10.3 WHAT FAITH-DEVELOPMENT WRITERS SAY

Some writers have described faith as moving through stages. James Fowler's *Stages of Faith* (1981) is the classic version. More recently, Brian McLaren's *Faith After Doubt* (2021) describes four stages he calls simplicity, complexity, perplexity, and harmony. In his model, the "perplexity" stage, where everything seems questionable, is a normal part of the journey rather than a failure.

These models are descriptions, not laws. Nobody is required to move through them in order, and you don't need to accept any of them to use the basic insight: the season you're in is a season, not your final state.

### 10.4 PRACTICING "BOTH"

A simple exercise: when you catch yourself putting something in a bin, try writing a "both" sentence instead.

- "My church was all bad" becomes "My church hurt me, *and* some people there were genuinely kind."
- "I was an idiot to believe that" becomes "I believed what I was taught as a child, *and* I'm allowed to change my mind as an adult."
- "Religious people are stupid" becomes "Some religious people I know are thoughtful, *and* some teachings did real harm."

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Where do you notice all-or-nothing thinking in yourself right now?
2. Write three "both" sentences about your own life.
3. If McLaren's stages are a useful map, where would you put yourself today?
