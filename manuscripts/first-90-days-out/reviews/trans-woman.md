# Sensitivity Read: *First 90 Days Out*

**Reader perspective:** trans woman, about 30, raised Southern Baptist, out to her family for two years, partly estranged, still unsure about God.

**Scope:** full manuscript (front matter, Parts One–Three, Appendices A–I). Quotes are exact. Severity scale: MUST FIX (could hurt or endanger this reader, or leaves out something she needs) / SHOULD FIX (she'd feel it was written for someone else) / NICE TO HAVE (polish).

---

## Overall verdict

I trusted this book more than almost anything else I've read on leaving church. It doesn't push me toward atheism or back to church, it cites its sources and admits where they're weak, and lines like "rules about gender that never fit you" and "the version of you your family loved" hit me hard. Seeing Trans Lifeline on the first page, and a trans author named in the front matter, told me someone had thought about people like me. That's also why the gaps hurt. After those first pages the book mostly forgets that a trans reader's body, family, safety, and medical care don't work like the default reader's. The purity-culture chapter gives women the girls' version and men the boys' version, and I was raised on the boys' version as a girl. The family chapter treats my parents' anger as fear in disguise but never mentions that they still call me by a name I don't use. The one script about kids has the reader saying "Mom and I," so it assumes I'm the dad. Safety means money and phone plans, not getting through Christmas Eve in my home church in one piece. The therapist chapter warns about conversion therapy aimed at sexual orientation but not the version aimed at gender identity, which is the one most likely to be offered to me. After 10 p.m., every crisis option for someone over 25 can involve police, and the book only says so about 988. None of this needs a new book. It needs about a dozen paragraphs written by someone who knows that trans men and trans women came out of these churches carrying different things. Fix the five MUST FIX notes and I'd hand this to every trans girl I know who's still in a pew.

## Notes

### 1. Crisis page: every after-hours option for adults over 25 can involve police, and only 988 says so
**Location:** "If You Need Help Right Now" (front matter) and Appendix A
**Quote:** "Trans Lifeline … Weekday hours only … It does not call emergency services without your consent." / "Crisis Text Line (US): text HOME to 741741."

**Reaction:** I'm 30, so Trevor and 988's press 3 aren't for me. Trans Lifeline is the only line here I'd actually trust, and the book tells me it's closed at the exact hour (2 a.m.) it tells me to expect the fear. You were honest that 988 can involve emergency services, but you didn't say the same about Crisis Text Line. For a trans woman, an involuntary hold can mean a men's ward and a stack of misgendered paperwork. I need to know that before I text.

**Suggested change:** Under the Crisis Text Line bullet, add: "Like 988, Crisis Text Line can contact emergency services if a counselor believes your life is in immediate danger." After the Trans Lifeline bullet, add a new bullet:
> "**If you want to talk to someone and you aren't in immediate danger:** peer-run 'warmlines' are free, non-emergency listening lines staffed by people with lived experience of mental health struggles. Warmline.org lists them by state; many run into the evening. If you're over 25 and wary of police involvement, a warmline or Trans Lifeline (during its hours) may feel safer than a crisis line. That's a reasonable thing to weigh. Please still reach out."

Add a matching row to the Appendix A table. Verify warmline.org and hours at final check, along with the other numbers.

**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 2. Chapter 3's safety checklist doesn't include the things a trans woman actually depends on
**Location:** Chapter 3, §3.1 "The Dependency Checklist," and the chapter as a whole
**Quote:** "Before the big questions: a roof, money, and a phone nobody else controls."

**Reaction:** This is a great chapter, and I'd have used it, but it was written for someone whose biggest risk is losing a paycheck. My risks are different. My birth certificate is in my mom's filing cabinet. My hormones depend on one prescriber and one pharmacy. My state's laws keep changing. And for me "safety" includes whether I'm physically safe at my cousin's wedding in the fellowship hall. None of that is on the list.

**Suggested change:** Add two items to the §3.1 checklist:
> "- **Documents.** Where are your birth certificate, Social Security card, and passport? Who has them? If you've changed your name or gender marker, or plan to, do you have certified copies somewhere you control?
> - **Medical care and prescriptions.** Who prescribes your medications, including hormones if you take them? Is your clinic or hospital religiously affiliated? Could a change in insurance or employer interrupt a prescription you can't safely stop?"

Then add a short new section:
> "### 3.5 IF YOU'RE TRANS OR VISIBLY QUEER
> For some readers, 'safety' isn't only money and housing. It's whether you're physically safe in certain rooms. Before a family event, a funeral, or a visit to your old church, ask: Will I have my own way home? Who there will back me up? What will I do if someone uses my old name or makes a scene? Laws on IDs, health care, and public facilities differ by state and are changing fast. A local LGBTQ+ community center or a legal-aid organization can tell you what applies where you live. Planning for this isn't paranoia. It's the same planning this chapter asks of everyone, applied to your actual risks."

**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. Purity culture gives women the girls' version and men the boys' version, and I got the boys' version as a girl
**Location:** Chapter 8, §8.2, last paragraph; and Week 7 prompts (Days 45 and 47)
**Quote:** "Purity culture didn't only affect women. Men describe being taught that they were one glance away from lust at all times … LGBTQ+ people were often taught that their bodies were a problem with no solution."

**Reaction:** At my church the boys learned to "bounce your eyes" and the girls learned to cover up so the boys wouldn't stumble. I got the first lesson and secretly belonged to the second. Now I'm a woman in a world that hands me the second lesson every day. One sentence about LGBTQ+ people is not enough for that. Then Day 47 asks me to "write about one way your body has taken care of you," and with dysphoria that prompt can feel like being asked to thank my jailer. It isn't wrong, but it needs a door I'm allowed to walk through.

**Suggested change:** After the §8.2 paragraph quoted above, add:
> "Trans people often carry a doubled version of this. A trans woman raised as a boy may have been trained in the 'every man's battle' script (her body as a weapon to be managed, her eyes as a danger) while privately identifying with the girls being told to cover up. A trans man may have been held responsible for other people's thoughts about a body he never felt was his. Then, after transition, many trans people find the *other* set of rules waiting for them. If that's you, you aren't unlearning one script. You're unlearning two, and possibly grieving that neither one was ever written for you."

(Source suggestion for the men's script, which the book now describes without a citation: Stephen Arterburn and Fred Stoeker, *Every Man's Battle* [WaterBrook, 2000], and *Every Young Man's Battle* [2002]. Verify the edition before print.)

In §8.3 under "Talk to the right professional," change "experience with clients from conservative religious backgrounds" to "experience with clients from conservative religious backgrounds, and, if it applies, with trans patients."

Add an alternative line under Day 45 and Day 47:
> "*If your relationship with your body is complicated by dysphoria, or by being raised as a gender you aren't, you can answer about the body you're building, or write instead about one thing you've done to make your body more yours.*"

**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 4. The scripts assume nobody misgenders you, and the kid script assumes you're the dad
**Location:** Appendix C, "To your kid"; Chapter 9, §9.2 table and scripts; §9.4
**Quote:** "Mom and I believe different things about God right now. That's okay."

**Reaction:** That script is spoken by the father. I've corrected my own family's pronouns for two years, and the book did the same thing to me on the scripts page. Meanwhile the conversation I'll actually have isn't "I've been going through some stuff with my faith." It's my dad saying "Son, this is what happens when you walk away from God," or my mom insisting my leaving church is "because of the trans thing." No script in the book covers either.

**Suggested change:**
- Appendix C, "To your kid": change to: "**[Your other parent / their name]** and I believe different things about God right now. That's okay. You can ask either of us anything." In §9.4, change "Mom and Dad believe different things about God" to "Your parents believe different things about God."
- Add to the §9.2 table:

| They say | They often mean |
|---|---|
| "This is because of the [gay / trans] thing." | "I need one explanation for all of this, and that's the one I can blame." |
| They use your old name or the wrong pronouns. | Sometimes fear, sometimes refusal. Either way, you can hold the line without debating it. |

- Add to Appendix C:
> **When someone uses your old name or the wrong pronouns**
> "My name is ___. I'll keep reminding you. If it keeps happening on purpose, I'm going to leave and we can try another time."

> **When someone says your faith and your identity are the same problem**
> "Those are two different things happening in my life. I'm willing to talk about either one. I'm not willing to have one used as proof against the other."

**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 5. The conversion therapy warning covers orientation rebrands but not gender-identity ones
**Location:** Chapter 13, §13.5 and §13.3
**Quote:** "sometimes under names that critics identify as rebrands, such as 'reintegrative therapy' or counseling for 'unwanted same-sex attraction.'"

**Reaction:** Every example here is about who I'd date. The version most likely to be offered to me, a trans woman whose family would happily pay for it, is marketed as neutral "exploration" of my gender. If I didn't already know the term, this section would let me walk right into it. Also, §13.3 never mentions that for many trans people, a therapist isn't only help. It can be a gatekeeper who decides whether I get a letter for care.

**Suggested change:** In §13.5, after "unwanted same-sex attraction," add: ", or, for gender identity, approaches marketed as 'gender exploratory therapy,' which critics and several professional bodies describe as gender identity change efforts under a new name (supporters dispute this)." Source it the way the rest of the book does. The APA's 2021 Gender Identity Change Efforts resolution is already cited, and a source documenting the "gender exploratory" critique should be added. Mark it CHECK BEFORE PRINT.

Add to the §13.3 questions:
> - "Have you worked with trans clients, including trans adults who transitioned years ago?"
> - "If I ever need a letter for gender-affirming care, do you write them, and what do you require first?"

and add after "A good therapist won't be offended by any of these": "If you're trans, you're allowed to keep your therapist and your letter-writer separate, so your mental health care isn't tied to your medical access."

**Severity:** MUST FIX

**Suggested change:** Keep both. Don't hedge or move the author line in future editions. (See note 7 for one sentence to add.)

### 7. The author is a trans man, and the book never admits that trans women's path out was different
**Location:** "A Note on Who Wrote This"
**Quote:** "He went through his own version of these ninety days."

**Reaction:** I'm glad he's trans. I also know a trans man raised as a girl in the same church got the modesty talks and the women's Bible study, while I got Royal Ambassadors and men's breakfasts. I got told I'd be the spiritual head of a household. His version wasn't mine. If the book just said so once, I'd trust everything after it more, not less.

**Suggested change:** After "not to suggest you should end up standing there too," add:
> "He also knows his own ninety days aren't everyone's. Trans women, cis women, gay men, people of color, and people raised in different denominations carried different rules out of the same churches. Where this book's examples don't fit you, that's a limit of the author's view, not a sign that your experience doesn't count."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. KEEP: "rules about gender that never fit you" and "the version of you your family loved"
**Location:** Chapter 6, §6.2 ("Return" pile); Chapter 7, §7.1
**Quote:** "the idea that doubt is sin, rules about gender that never fit you" / "your place in your family, or the version of you your family loved"

**Reaction:** I had to put the book down after the second one. My parents do love me, but they loved the son more, and they still talk about him like he moved away. Nobody has ever written that loss down for me as a loss. These two lines did more for me than the rest of Part Two combined.

**Suggested change:** Keep both exactly. Consider repeating the second line as the Day 38 or Day 41 prompt, e.g., Day 41: "What future did you imagine for yourself at sixteen? What's left of it? *If the person your family imagined isn't you, you can grieve their version and still keep yours.*"

**Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep as is)

### 9. No section for people who are already estranged
**Location:** Chapter 5, §5.2; Chapter 9, §9.1–9.2
**Quote:** "I know you're scared for me. I'm okay. I'd like us to keep having dinner together even though we disagree."

**Reaction:** The whole family chapter assumes there's still a weekly dinner to protect. For me, the faith conversation is landing on a relationship that already broke once, when I came out. The safety map has "Not yet" and "Maybe never," but nothing for "we're barely speaking and I don't know if I want that to change." Partial estrangement is extremely common for trans people, and the book acts as if it doesn't exist.

**Suggested change:** Add §9.6:
> "### 9.6 IF YOU'RE ALREADY ESTRANGED
> Some readers aren't deciding how to tell their family. The relationship already broke, often over coming out, before faith was even on the table. If that's you, you get to decide what kind of contact you want: none, limited (holidays, texts, one parent and not the other), or slowly rebuilt. All of those are legitimate. Estrangement is not a failure to forgive, and contact is not a betrayal of yourself. You don't owe your family a faith announcement to go with everything else. If they hear about it secondhand, you can say: 'That's true. I'm not ready to talk about it with you yet.'"

Add a workbook option under Day 53: "*If you're not in contact with a parent, you can write the conversation you'd want if contact were safe, or write about why it isn't.*"

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. Going back to church to grieve, or for a holiday, gets no safety warning for visibly trans readers
**Location:** Chapter 7, §7.3; Chapter 9, §9.5
**Quote:** "go to one last service on purpose, sit in the back, and leave before the end"

**Reaction:** That ritual is lovely, and I could never do it in my home church. Half the congregation knew me as the deacon's son. Sitting in the back wouldn't make me invisible; it would make me a sermon illustration. The holiday section says "have an exit plan," which is good, but for me the exit plan is about physical safety and getting home on my own, not just tension.

**Suggested change:** After the §7.3 list, add: "If you're trans or visibly queer, think about whether your old congregation is safe to sit in. A different church of the same denomination in another town, or a recorded service at home, can carry the same weight." In §9.5, after "have an exit plan if things get tense," add: "If you're trans, that plan may need to include your own transportation, a friend you can call, and a decision ahead of time about what you'll do if someone uses your old name."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. "Affirming" and "welcome LGBTQ+ people" don't tell a trans woman what she needs to ask
**Location:** Chapter 12, §12.2; Appendix B, "Affirming"
**Quote:** "many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people. Ask directly about their stance before you invest"

**Reaction:** I've been to "welcoming" churches where the gay couple ran the coffee hour and the women's retreat still wasn't sure about me. "Ask directly" is good advice. Give me the actual questions, because the general one gets a general answer.

**Suggested change:** After "Ask directly about their stance before you invest," add: "If you're trans, ask specifically: Are trans women welcome in women's groups and leadership? Will you use my name and pronouns in the bulletin, the directory, and prayer requests? Which restroom would you expect me to use?" In Appendix B, change the "Affirming" definition to: "A church or group that fully includes LGBTQ+ people, including in leadership and marriage, and recognizes trans people as the gender they are."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 12. Catholicism and Orthodoxy are listed as landing places without saying they officially don't affirm transition
**Location:** Chapter 14, §14.2, "A different kind of Christianity"
**Quote:** "They move to mainline Protestant churches … to Catholicism or Orthodoxy, or to progressive evangelical communities."

**Reaction:** The book is careful everywhere else to tell me what's official and what varies. Here it lists churches as alternatives without telling me that some of them, at the top, consider my transition a problem. Someone at my stage could find a kind priest, invest a year, and then run into the catechism.

**Suggested change:** After that sentence, add: "Positions on gender and sexuality vary a great deal among these. The Catholic Church's official teaching, restated in the 2024 declaration *Dignitas Infinita*, rejects gender transition, and the Orthodox churches generally hold similar positions, though individual parishes and clergy differ. Mainline denominations range from fully affirming to divided. If this matters to you, ask before you invest." Add *Dignitas Infinita* (Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, April 2024) to the Chapter 14 sources and mark it CHECK BEFORE PRINT.

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. The "go deeper" guides point LGBTQ+ readers to passages about sexuality, not gender
**Location:** Chapter 1, §1.3; Appendix E; Appendix I
**Quote:** "*Unbound* for the passages used against LGBTQ+ people" / "the seven passages used against LGBTQ+ Christians … which brought the word 'homosexuals' into a major English Bible."

**Reaction:** The verses I heard weren't Leviticus 18. They were "male and female he created them" and Deuteronomy 22:5 about men wearing women's clothing. If *Unbound* covers those, say so. If it doesn't, don't say "LGBTQ+" when you mean "LGB," because I'll buy it expecting help with my hell fear and find out I wasn't in it.

**Suggested change:** Check what *Unbound* actually covers. If it doesn't treat Genesis 1:27 and Deuteronomy 22:5, change the Appendix E/I wording to "the seven passages used against gay, lesbian, and bisexual Christians," and add one sentence: "The verses most often used against trans people (such as Genesis 1:27 and Deuteronomy 22:5) are a different conversation; [future guide / recommended reading]." Ideally, Chapter 4 also gets a one-line acknowledgment that for many LGBTQ+ readers the hell fear is about *who they are*, not only what they believe: "For many LGBTQ+ readers, the fear isn't abstract. It's 'I'm going to hell for being who I am.' The same first aid applies, and that fear deserves its own daylight."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 14. "How you were raised" is missing from the operating-system list, and so is "what kind of man or woman you'd be"
**Location:** Chapter 1, §1.2 and §1.3
**Quote:** "who you could date, and what you could do on the date" / "**A person.** A gay friend, a sibling who came out"

**Reaction:** For a Southern Baptist kid, church decided whether you went to Royal Ambassadors or Girls in Action. It decided whether you'd lead your home someday or submit in it, and what to do with your voice, your hair, your hands. That was the deepest layer of the operating system for me, and the list skips it. And in "how it usually starts," transition is never named, even though for a lot of us it's the whole reason.

**Suggested change:** Add to the §1.2 list: "- what it meant to be a man or a woman, and which one you were told you were." In §1.3 under "Your own body," change to: "Coming out, transitioning, a divorce, an illness, depression that didn't lift when you prayed harder."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 15. Dating gets one line in the operating system and nothing after that
**Location:** Chapter 8, §8.3, "Your timeline is yours"
**Quote:** "Safety and consent still matter, for you as much as anyone."

**Reaction:** I left the church at 28 with no dating experience as a woman. Every rule I was taught was for a boy courting a girl. Dating as a trans woman comes with its own risks: when to disclose, who's fetishizing you, who might get violent. "Safety and consent still matter" is true, but it's a seatbelt sign on an airplane I don't know how to fly.

**Suggested change:** Add a bullet to §8.3:
> "- **Dating after purity culture.** Many people leave with no adult experience of dating at all, or only experience under rules that never fit them. Go slower than your loneliness wants. Meet new people in public, tell a friend where you'll be, and trust the part of you that notices when someone is more interested in an idea of you than in you. Trans readers in particular may want to think ahead about when and how to disclose. A local LGBTQ+ center or support group is a good place to hear how other people handle it."

**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 16. Childhood prompts can open dysphoria without warning
**Location:** Day 3, Day 41, Day 77
**Quote:** "Describe your Sunday mornings as a kid in as much sensory detail as you can: smells, sounds, what you wore."

**Reaction:** What I wore was a clip-on tie and a little blazer, and on Day 3 of a book I'm just learning to trust, I don't want to be ambushed by that. These prompts are good. They just need the same permission the book gives everywhere else.

**Suggested change:** Add a line to "Write in This Book" in the front matter: "Some prompts ask about your childhood. If your childhood self was raised as someone you aren't, you can answer from where you stand now, skip the detail that hurts, or skip the day. The book means it about no streaks." Day 77 ("your sixteen-year-old self") could add "*or the self you were hiding at sixteen*."

## MUST FIX summary

1. Crisis page / Appendix A: say that Crisis Text Line can also contact emergency services, and add a non-emergency warmline option for adults over 25 when Trans Lifeline is closed.
2. Chapter 3: add documents, prescriptions and hormones, and physical safety for trans and visibly queer readers to the dependency checklist and a new §3.5.
3. Chapter 8 / Days 45 and 47: name the doubled purity-culture script that trans women raised as boys carry, and give dysphoria-aware alternatives to the body prompts.
4. Appendix C / Chapter 9: fix the "Mom and I" kid script that assumes the reader is a father, and add scripts for deadnaming and misgendering and for "it's because of the trans thing."
5. Chapter 13: name gender-identity conversion-therapy rebrands ("gender exploratory therapy"), and add therapist questions about trans clients and letter gatekeeping.
