# PART THREE · DAYS 61–90

## BUILD SOMETHING ON PURPOSE

Two months in. You've steadied the ground and looked hard at what you were given. Part Three is about choosing: how you'll decide what's right, who your people are, what help you need, and where you might land.

None of these chapters is a final answer. They're the first pieces of a life you get to build on purpose, maybe for the first time.

---

## 11 · ETHICS WITHOUT THE OLD RULEBOOK

*Being good was never only about following rules.*

### 11.1 THE FEAR

One of the most common fears people have after leaving a strict faith is: *If I don't believe the rules came from God, what stops me from becoming a terrible person?* Sometimes other people say it to you directly: "Without God, how do you know what's right?"

It's a fair question. It also has a long and serious history of answers, and almost none of them involve becoming a terrible person.

### 11.2 HOW PEOPLE HAVE THOUGHT ABOUT RIGHT AND WRONG

Philosophers in many cultures have worked on ethics for thousands of years, often without relying on any particular religion. Very roughly, the main Western traditions ask different questions:

| Approach | The basic question | Associated thinkers |
|---|---|---|
| **Virtue ethics** | What kind of person do I want to become? | Aristotle |
| **Duty-based ethics** | What would be right for anyone to do in my position? | Immanuel Kant |
| **Consequence-based ethics** | Which choice leads to the least harm and the most good? | Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill |
| **Care ethics** | What do the people in front of me need, given our relationships? | Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings |

Religious people use these too. Christianity has its own long tradition of virtue ethics, especially through Thomas Aquinas. You don't need to choose one approach. Most people use some mix without knowing the names.

### 11.3 WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW

You probably have more ethical equipment than you think. You know that hurting people for fun is wrong. You know that keeping promises matters. You know that the kid who gets bullied deserves defending. You knew those things in church, and you know them now. The rules didn't create those convictions. At best, they gave them a vocabulary.

### 11.4 WHAT CHANGES

What may change is how you handle the harder cases: the ones where your old rulebook gave an answer that never sat right with you. Who you can love. How to treat people from other religions. Whether doubt is a sin. Leaving gives you the room to think about those cases freshly. It also means you have to do the thinking yourself. That can feel heavy. It's also a kind of respect for yourself.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. List five things you're sure are right or wrong, with or without God.
2. Which of the four approaches in 11.2 sounds most like how you already make decisions?
3. Name one rule from your old church that you used to follow and now think was wrong. What do you think instead?

## 12 · FINDING YOUR PEOPLE

*Church did one thing really well. You'll have to build it on purpose now.*

### 12.1 WHAT CHURCH PROVIDED

Whatever else it did, church usually gave people a ready-made community: people who saw you every week, knew your name, brought meals when you were sick, and showed up to your kid's graduation. Many people don't realize how much of their social life ran through church until they leave and their calendar goes silent.

That isn't a small thing. In May 2023 the US Surgeon General, Dr. Vivek Murthy, issued an advisory on what he called an epidemic of loneliness and isolation. It describes social connection as "a fundamental human need" with measurable effects on health. Losing your community at the same time as your beliefs is a double hit.

### 12.2 WHERE PEOPLE FIND COMMUNITY AFTERWARD

There's no single replacement, and most people end up with a patchwork. Options people often use:

- **Recurring activities.** A climbing gym, a choir, a running club, a board game night, a volunteer shift. The key is that it happens every week with the same people.
- **Secular gatherings.** Some cities have groups that meet on Sundays for music, talks, and community without religion.
- **Affirming or progressive congregations.** For people who want to keep some form of faith practice, many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people. Ask directly about their stance before you invest; a church's website language can be vague.
- **Other traditions.** Some people find community among Quakers, Unitarian Universalists, Buddhist meditation groups, or Jewish communities that welcome interested outsiders.
- **Online communities.** Forums, Discord servers, and podcast communities for people leaving religion. These can be a lifeline, especially in small towns. Watch for spaces where anger at religion becomes the only topic; they can keep you stuck.
- **Support groups.** Peer support groups for people recovering from religious harm exist both in person and online.

### 12.3 WHY IT TAKES SO LONG

Friendship research suggests it takes a lot of hours together before an acquaintance becomes a close friend. In one widely cited study, the communication researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated that it took more than 200 hours together, on average, to become close friends. Church gave you those hours automatically. Now you have to choose them. That's why "join something weekly" is more useful advice than "make friends."

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Who are your people right now? List them honestly, even if the list is short.
2. Pick one recurring activity you could try in the next two weeks.
3. What did your best friendship at church give you? Where else might you find that?

## 13 · CHOOSING HELP

*Not every counselor is safe for someone in your situation.*

### 13.1 WHEN TO LOOK FOR A THERAPIST

You don't have to be in crisis to deserve therapy. People see therapists to understand themselves, work through grief, handle family conflict, or untangle anxiety. If anything from Chapter 2's warning list has lasted more than a couple of weeks, please don't wait.

### 13.2 KNOW WHAT YOU'RE GETTING

In the US, the words "counselor" and "therapist" can mean very different things.

**Licensed mental health professionals** have a graduate degree, supervised clinical hours, and a state license. They're accountable to a licensing board. Common credentials:

| Credential | What it means |
|---|---|
| **LPC / LMHC** | Licensed Professional Counselor / Licensed Mental Health Counselor (titles vary by state, e.g., LPCC in California) |
| **LMFT** | Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist |
| **LCSW** | Licensed Clinical Social Worker |
| **Psychologist (PhD or PsyD)** | Doctoral degree in psychology plus a state license. Look for the words "licensed psychologist." |
| **Psychiatrist (MD or DO)** | Medical doctor with specialty training in psychiatry; can prescribe medication |
| **PMHNP** | Psychiatric-mental health nurse practitioner; can prescribe in most states |

**"Christian counseling"** can mean a licensed therapist who is also a Christian and integrates faith when the client wants it. Some are excellent.

**"Biblical counseling"** (also called "nouthetic counseling," a movement launched by Jay Adams's 1970 book *Competent to Counsel*) usually means something different: counseling based on the view that Scripture alone is sufficient for emotional problems. Many biblical counselors are certified by religious organizations rather than licensed by the state, which means they may not be bound by the same professional and ethical rules. If you're leaving a faith, a counselor whose goal is to bring you back to it is not neutral help.

### 13.3 QUESTIONS TO ASK A THERAPIST

Before you book a full session, you can ask for a short phone call. Questions to ask:

- "Are you licensed? In which state?"
- "Have you worked with clients leaving a religious community, or recovering from religious harm?"
- "If I decide to stay religious, or decide not to be, will you support either direction?"
- "Have you worked with LGBTQ+ clients?" (if relevant)
- "How do you handle it if a client's goals conflict with your own beliefs?"

A good therapist won't be offended by any of these. If someone is, that tells you what you need to know.

### 13.4 IF MONEY IS TIGHT

- Many therapists offer sliding-scale fees based on income.
- Community mental health centers and university training clinics often cost less.
- If you have insurance, your provider directory lists in-network therapists.
- Some secular organizations maintain directories of therapists who are experienced with religious trauma and have committed not to impose religious views on clients.

### 13.5 A WORD ON CONVERSION THERAPY

If you're LGBTQ+, be aware that "conversion therapy" (efforts to change sexual orientation or gender identity) is still offered in some religious settings, sometimes under names that critics identify as rebrands, such as "reintegrative therapy" or counseling for "unwanted same-sex attraction." Major US medical and mental health associations oppose it, including the American Psychological Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics.

The legal picture is changing. In March 2026, in *Chiles v. Salazar*, the US Supreme Court ruled 8–1 that Colorado's ban, as applied to a counselor's talk therapy, restricts speech and must meet a stricter First Amendment test. It sent the case back to the lower courts, and similar laws in other states are being challenged. The ruling was about free speech. It did not find conversion therapy safe or effective. Laws differ by state and country, so check the current situation where you live.

2. Which questions from 13.3 matter most to you? Add your own.
3. Write down one concrete step: a directory to search, a call to make, an insurance card to look at.

## 14 · WHERE PEOPLE LAND

*Short, fair portraits. No ranking.*

### 14.1 YOU'RE IN A BIG CROWD

The share of American adults who don't identify with any religion grew a great deal from the 1990s through the 2010s, and has recently leveled off. The Pew Research Center now counts roughly three in ten US adults as religious "nones" (29 percent in its 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study): atheists, agnostics, and people who describe their religion as "nothing in particular." Many others have switched to a different religious tradition, or changed how they practice while keeping a religious identity.

Whatever you decide, you won't be alone.

### 14.2 THE MAIN DESTINATIONS

These are portraits of where people commonly end up, not recommendations. Most people mix and match, and many move between them over a lifetime.

**A different kind of Christianity.** Some people find that what they left wasn't Christianity itself but one strict version of it. They move to mainline Protestant churches (Episcopal, Lutheran ELCA, Presbyterian PC(USA), United Church of Christ, and others), to Catholicism or Orthodoxy, or to progressive evangelical communities. They may hold the Bible as sacred while reading it differently. The guides at What They Skipped exist partly for this group.

**Another tradition.** Some people find a home in Judaism (often through conversion, which takes time and study), Buddhism, Quakerism, Unitarian Universalism, or other traditions. Some are drawn by practice more than belief.

**Spiritual but not religious.** People who still sense something sacred or transcendent but don't want an institution. They may meditate, spend time in nature, or keep personal rituals.

**Agnostic.** People who believe the question of God can't be settled, or who simply haven't settled it. Agnosticism isn't indecision. For many it's a considered position.

**Atheist or humanist.** People who don't believe in any god. Many find meaning in humanism: the idea that people can lead ethical, meaningful lives grounded in reason, compassion, and shared human experience.

**Undecided, for now.** A real category, and an honest one. You can live a full life without having filed your final answer.

### 14.3 A THING TO WATCH FOR

Wherever you land, watch for any group, religious or not, that asks you to stop asking questions, cut off people who disagree, or hand your judgment over to a leader. Those are the habits of high-control systems, and they can show up anywhere.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Which portrait in 14.2 sounds closest to where you are today? Which sounds furthest?
2. Which one were you taught to fear most? Why?
3. Is there one you're curious about? What's one small way to learn more about it?

## 15 · DAY 90 AND AFTER

*You made it through the first season. There will be others.*

### 15.1 LOOKING BACK

Ninety days isn't long enough to resolve the biggest questions anyone can ask. It's long enough to get your footing, notice what you're carrying, and choose some first steps. If you did that, even partly, you did the work.

Take out your Day 1 snapshot. Read it slowly. You might notice that you believe something different now. You might notice you believe exactly the same things, but they scare you less. Both are progress.

### 15.2 WHAT TENDS TO COME BACK

Some things come back after the first season, and it helps to expect them:

- **Holidays.** Christmas and Easter can reopen the grief for years. Plan for them.
- **Life events.** Weddings, funerals, births, and illness can bring old questions back with force. A funeral is a hard place to be a skeptic, and also a hard place to be a believer.
- **Old fears.** The hell fear, or the "what if I'm wrong" loop, may return under stress. It usually comes back quieter, and you'll know what to do with it now.
- **Family changes.** Relationships keep moving. Some get easier. Some don't.

None of these means you've failed or gone backward. They're the weather of a long journey.

### 15.3 THE NEXT NINETY DAYS

You can do another ninety. Some people restart the workbook from Day 1 and are surprised at how differently they answer. Others pick a single area, such as family, community, or the body, and work on that for the next season. Some put the book on a shelf and live. All of those are fine.

### WRITE IN THIS BOOK

1. Write a letter to your Day 1 self. What do you want them to know?
2. What are you still carrying? What have you put down?
3. What's one thing you want to work on in the next ninety days?
