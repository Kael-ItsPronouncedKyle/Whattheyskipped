# Sensitivity Read: Nonbinary Reader

**Manuscript:** *First 90 Days Out* (files 01–04)
**Reader profile:** nonbinary (they/them), late 20s, raised in a conservative PCA Presbyterian family with strict complementarian teaching, now living in a city, in therapy, partnered.

## Overall verdict

This is a kind, careful, well-sourced book, and the parts written in plain second person often worked on me without my having to translate. "Rules about gender that never fit you" in the Return pile, the trans-run crisis line on page one, conversion therapy defined to include gender identity, and a Day 90 letter that calls my past self "them" all felt like someone had considered me. But the word "nonbinary" never appears, "transgender" turns up only in citation titles and a crisis-line name, and the one teaching that shaped my childhood more than hell did, complementarianism (biblical manhood and womanhood), gets one glossary line and nothing else. When gender does come up, it comes up as a binary. Purity culture is about "women" and "men," with "LGBTQ+ people" added in one closing sentence. Families are "Mom and Dad." A script hands me the line "Mom and I." Because the book comes from a trans author and an LGBTQ+-centred publisher, these gaps stand out more than they would elsewhere. Readers like me will notice that a book about what churches skipped has skipped us. The fixes are mostly small: a few word swaps, one new section on gender roles, and some practical lines on names, pronouns, documents and insurance. With them the book would go from not hostile to clearly for me too. As it stands I'd recommend it to my partner sooner than to my nonbinary friends.

## Notes

### 1. Nonbinary people are never named
- **Location:** Whole manuscript. Closest instances: Front matter crisis page, "Trans Lifeline (peer support run by trans people)"; Ch. 3.2, "If you're LGBTQ+ and worried about being kicked out"
- **Reaction:** I searched for myself and found only the "+". "Nonbinary" appears nowhere, and "transgender" appears only in citation titles. I assume the author knows people like me exist, but the book never tells me it knows.
- **Suggested change:** Name nonbinary people explicitly at least in the crisis page, Ch. 3.2, Ch. 8, Ch. 13.3 and the glossary (see the notes below). On the crisis page, change the Trans Lifeline line to: "**Trans Lifeline (peer support run by trans people, including nonbinary people):** …" Add glossary entries:
  > **Nonbinary.** A person whose gender isn't only "man" or only "woman." Many nonbinary people use they/them pronouns; some use others.
  > **Transgender.** A person whose gender differs from the sex they were assigned at birth. Some nonbinary people describe themselves as trans; some don't.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 2. Complementarianism gets one glossary line and no chapter
- **Location:** Appendix B, Glossary: "Complementarians hold that men and women have different God-given roles, with men leading in church and home." No other mention anywhere.
- **Reaction:** In the PCA this wasn't one doctrine among many. It was the structure of everything: who prayed at dinner, who could ask a question in Sunday school, which room I was sent to at retreats, what my future was supposed to be. The book covers hell, purity culture and black-and-white thinking but never the gender-role system. That system sorted me into a role I've spent ten years climbing out of, and it is often the hardest thing for nonbinary and trans exvangelicals to put down.
- **Suggested change:** Add a section, either 8.4 or a short standalone chapter in Part Two, titled "Biblical Manhood and Womanhood." Draft:
  > ### 8.4 THE ROLES YOU WERE ASSIGNED
  > Many evangelical and Reformed churches teach **complementarianism**: the view that God made men and women different in ways that assign them different roles, with men leading in the home and church and women supporting that leadership. The Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood set out the view in the Danvers Statement (1987). In 2017 the same organization published the Nashville Statement, which also says that adopting a transgender self-conception is inconsistent with God's purposes. Some denominations have formally endorsed it.
  >
  > If you grew up inside this, gender roles may have shaped more of your life than any single doctrine: who prayed out loud, who could teach, which retreat you went to, what you were told your future would hold. For many people the roles were a tight fit. For some they never fit at all. If you're transgender or nonbinary, you may have spent years being trained for a role that wasn't yours, and being told that the mismatch was rebellion.
  >
  > Unlearning this can mean noticing small automatic things: waiting for a man in the room to decide, feeling guilty for being "too much" or "not enough" of something, flinching at being called "sir" or "ma'am." It can also mean grieving a version of yourself you were never allowed to try.

  Source the Danvers and Nashville Statements (cbmw.org), and verify any denominational endorsement (for example, the PCA General Assembly's 2019 action on the Nashville Statement) before naming a denomination. Add a matching Week 7 prompt (see note 12).
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. Purity culture is described as women's and men's experience, with LGBTQ+ people added at the end
- **Location:** Ch. 8.2: "Purity culture didn't only affect women. Men describe being taught that they were one glance away from lust at all times… LGBTQ+ people were often taught that their bodies were a problem with no solution."
- **Reaction:** I was sent to the girls' modesty talk with a body I didn't experience as a girl's. I was taught to guard it for a future husband I was never going to be a wife to. My shame about sex and my discomfort with my body got so tangled I'm still sorting them out in therapy. One sentence that puts me inside "LGBTQ+" and calls me "a problem with no solution" doesn't describe that. It reads almost like a restatement of the church's verdict.
- **Suggested change:** Replace the final paragraph of 8.2 with:
  > Purity culture didn't only affect women. Men describe being taught that they were one glance away from lust at all times, and that their bodies were enemies to be managed. Lesbian, gay and bisexual people were often taught that their desires had no faithful outlet at all. Transgender and nonbinary people were sorted into one side of a strict boy/girl script, given the modesty rules or the "guard your eyes" rules for a gender that wasn't theirs, and told that the mismatch itself was sin. For some, shame about sex and distress about gender got wound together so tightly that it takes time to tell which is which.
  >
  > The research in this chapter studied women. We know of no comparable study of transgender or nonbinary people raised in purity culture. That is a gap in the research, not evidence that the harm was smaller.

  (Check the second paragraph against current literature before print. If a study exists, cite it instead.)
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 4. "Mom and I" in the script for talking to your kid
- **Location:** Appendix C, "To your kid": "Mom and I believe different things about God right now."
- **Reaction:** This script assumes I'm a dad married to a mom. Every other script in the appendix is gender-neutral, so this one stands out, and nonbinary parents, gay dads, moms married to women and solo co-parents are all written out in a single line.
- **Suggested change:** Replace with:
  > "[Name/Mama/Papa] and I believe different things about God right now. That's okay. You can ask either of us anything."

  Or simply: "Your other parent and I believe different things about God right now…"
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 5. "Mom and Dad" in the kids section
- **Location:** Ch. 9.4: "Be honest at their level: 'Mom and Dad believe different things about God, and that's okay.'"
- **Reaction:** This is the same assumption as note 4, in the main text. Children of nonbinary parents often have a name for that parent that isn't Mom or Dad, and families that don't match the picture will notice it.
- **Suggested change:** Replace with:
  > Be honest at their level: "We believe different things about God, and that's okay."
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 6. The two maps: coming out about faith and about gender at the same time
- **Location:** Ch. 5.1–5.2, "Rank people by safety, not by closeness."
- **Reaction:** The safety map is one of the most useful things in the book, and it describes my life well. But for me, and for many queer exvangelicals, "telling people" meant two disclosures that my family treated as one: leaving the PCA and being nonbinary. My parents still believe the first caused the second, or the other way round. The book never mentions that the two can be fused.
- **Suggested change:** Add after the table in 5.2:
  > If you're also coming out about your sexuality or gender, you may need two maps, or a map with two columns. Some people are safe for one conversation and not the other. Some families will treat the two as one story ("You only left because you're gay," or "You only think you're nonbinary because you left"). You're allowed to decide the order, and to keep one private while sharing the other.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 7. Families who won't use your name or pronouns
- **Location:** Ch. 9.2, the "They say / They often mean" table and scripts; Ch. 7.1: "your place in your family, or the version of you your family loved"
- **Reaction:** "The version of you your family loved" hit hard; it's accurate. But the parents section has no script for the thing that actually ends my family visits: being deadnamed and called "she" at the dinner table, often framed as a matter of conscience ("We can't lie to you").
- **Suggested change:** Add a row to the 9.2 table:
  > | "We love you too much to call you that." | "I'm afraid that agreeing means I've failed God, or you." |

  Add a script:
  > "I'm not asking you to agree with me about God. I'm asking you to call me by my name. If that can't happen today, I'm going to go, and we can try again another time."

  Add to the 7.1 losses list: "being called by your name, if your family won't use the one you go by now."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. Practical safety leaves out documents and the insurance paper trail
- **Location:** Ch. 3.1, the Dependency Checklist: "Health insurance. Is it through a religious employer or a parent's plan?"
- **Reaction:** This chapter is good, practical and unsentimental. But for trans and nonbinary people the biggest practical risks are about paperwork. A parent who is the policyholder can see therapy and gender-affirming care on benefits statements, and families sometimes keep birth certificates and passports. Those things matter if you need to move out quickly or update a name.
- **Suggested change:** Expand the Health insurance bullet and add a new one:
  > **Health insurance.** Is it through a religious employer or a parent's plan? If you're on a parent's plan, the policyholder may receive statements listing your appointments, including therapy or gender-affirming care. Some states let you request that these go to you instead; ask the insurer.
  > **Documents.** Who holds your birth certificate, passport, and Social Security card? If you might need to leave, or to change your name or gender marker, you'll want these in your own hands.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 9. Black-and-white thinking leaves out the binary I was most drilled in
- **Location:** Ch. 10.1: "Saved or lost. Heaven or hell. Truth or lies. God's way or the world's way."
- **Reaction:** "Male and female he created them" was the most absolute binary I was taught, and the one I had to unlearn about myself rather than about the world. The chapter's insight (people keep the bins and swap the labels) applies here very directly, and it's absent.
- **Suggested change:** Change the list to: "Saved or lost. Heaven or hell. Truth or lies. Male or female. God's way or the world's way." Add to the 10.4 "both" examples:
  > "I have to be one thing or the other" becomes "I was taught there were only two boxes, *and* I'm allowed to find out what's actually true about me."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. Therapist questions should ask about trans and nonbinary clients specifically
- **Location:** Ch. 13.3: "'Have you worked with LGBTQ+ clients?' (if relevant)"
- **Reaction:** I've sat with two "LGBTQ-friendly" therapists who were fine with my partner and lost with my pronouns. "LGBTQ+" on a profile often means LGB. The checklist is a great tool and should help readers ask the question that actually filters.
- **Suggested change:** Replace with:
  > - "Have you worked with LGBTQ+ clients, including transgender and nonbinary clients?" (if relevant)
  > - "Will you use my name and pronouns, including in your notes?"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. Affirming churches and groups: ask about trans and nonbinary people specifically
- **Location:** Ch. 12.2: "many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people. Ask directly about their stance"; Appendix B: "**Affirming.** A church or group that fully includes LGBTQ+ people, including in leadership and marriage."
- **Reaction:** The advice to ask directly is right. In practice "affirming" can mean affirming gay marriage while still running men's and women's ministries and choosing "brothers and sisters" for every liturgy. Nonbinary readers going to a PC(USA) or Episcopal church (Ch. 14.2) need to know what to ask.
- **Suggested change:** Add to 12.2:
  > "Affirming" doesn't always mean the same thing. If you're transgender or nonbinary, ask specifically: Do you welcome trans and nonbinary people in leadership? Are small groups and retreats split by gender? Will people use my name and pronouns?

  In the glossary, add: "Some churches are affirming of gay and lesbian members but not of transgender or nonbinary people; ask about both."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 12. Workbook prompts on the body and the future should include the gendered script
- **Location:** Day 41: "What future did you imagine for yourself at sixteen?"; Day 45: "What were you taught about your body before you turned twelve?"; Day 3: "Describe your Sunday mornings as a kid… what you wore."
- **Reaction:** At sixteen I didn't imagine a future; I was handed one (a husband, homeschooling, a quiet place in the church). Day 3's "what you wore" brought back the Sunday dress fights instantly. I don't want that prompt cut, because remembering it was useful, but the book never acknowledges what it might stir up.
- **Suggested change:**
  - Day 41: "What future did you imagine for yourself at sixteen, or what future were you told to want? What's left of it?"
  - Add to Week 7 (for example, as part of Day 45 or as a replacement for Day 47 if space is tight): "What were you taught a 'real man' or a 'godly woman' was supposed to be? Where did you fit, and where didn't you?"
  - Day 3: add "(If what you were made to wear is a hard memory, you can write about that too, or skip it.)"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. Group track ground rules should include names and pronouns
- **Location:** Appendix G: "What's said in the group stays in the group. / Nobody has to share their workbook pages…"
- **Reaction:** A peer group of exvangelicals is where I'd be most likely to be misgendered by someone kind, and least able to correct them in the moment. A single ground rule handles that without making it a big deal.
- **Suggested change:** Add a ground rule:
  > - Use the names and pronouns people give you. Go-around introductions can include pronouns, but no one has to share them.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 14. Conversion therapy section: name how it reaches gender identity
- **Location:** Ch. 13.5: "sometimes under names that critics identify as rebrands, such as 'reintegrative therapy' or counseling for 'unwanted same-sex attraction.'"
- **Reaction:** I'm glad gender identity is in the definition (see note 17). But both example rebrands are about orientation. In my world the pressure on gender came as "counseling for gender confusion" or "embracing your God-given sex," and readers like me might not recognise it as the same thing.
- **Suggested change:** Add after the first sentence:
  > Efforts aimed at gender identity are often described in other terms, such as helping someone "embrace their biological sex" or resolve "gender confusion."

  If the author wants a named rebrand for gender identity efforts, source it carefully before including it, because the terminology is contested.
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 15. The partners section assumes a believing spouse
- **Location:** Ch. 9.3: "If you're married to someone who still believes…"
- **Reaction:** My partner never believed, and we aren't married. My family's problem isn't that my partner is a believer; it's that they won't acknowledge my partner at all. The section only covers the mixed-faith marriage case.
- **Suggested change:** Add a short paragraph:
  > Your situation may look different: a partner who never believed, a relationship your family doesn't recognize, or a partner your family blames for your leaving. Those are real strains too. It's reasonable to agree with your partner ahead of time on how you'll handle family events, and what you each need if a visit goes badly.
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 16. Small additions to the "what you don't have to know" and symptom lists
- **Location:** Ch. 1.4, "You don't have to know whether God exists"; Ch. 2.1, "anxiety around sex, bodies, or pleasure"
- **Reaction:** These lists are calming and I want them kept. Some readers will be questioning gender at the same moment they're questioning God, and a single line would tell them they're not getting ahead of themselves.
- **Suggested change:**
  - Ch. 1.4: add "You don't have to have settled every other question about yourself either, including ones about sexuality or gender."
  - Ch. 2.1: change to "anxiety around sex, bodies, pleasure, or gender, even when they no longer believe the rules they were taught."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 17. KEEP: "Rules about gender that never fit you"
- **Location:** Ch. 6.2, Return pile: "rules about gender that never fit you"
- **Reaction:** This is the single line in the book where I felt seen. It's precise, it doesn't make a speech, and putting it next to "the idea that doubt is sin" treats it as an ordinary thing to return rather than a special case. It's also the natural place to point forward to the new section in note 2.
- **Suggested change:** Keep as written. Optionally add a cross-reference: "(See 8.4.)"
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep)

### 18. KEEP: The crisis page and the conversion-therapy definition
- **Location:** Front matter, "IF YOU NEED HELP RIGHT NOW"; Ch. 13.5: "efforts to change sexual orientation or gender identity"; sources cite the APA's *Resolution on Gender Identity Change Efforts*
- **Reaction:** Putting Trans Lifeline on the first page, noting that it won't call emergency services without consent, and explaining 988's LGBTQ+ option is careful work that matters to people like me. Many queer people avoid crisis lines because of the risk of police involvement. Including gender identity in the conversion therapy definition, with the APA's gender identity resolution cited, is what I'd expect from this publisher's scholarly standard.
- **Suggested change:** Keep both. Only add "including nonbinary people" to the Trans Lifeline line (note 1).
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep)

### 19. KEEP: The second-person voice and the singular "them"
- **Location:** Throughout; Ch. 15: "Write a letter to your Day 1 self. What do you want them to know?"
- **Reaction:** Because almost everything is addressed to "you," I seldom had to work out whether I was included, and the gendered moments stood out mainly because they're rare. The singular "them" for my own past self was a small, unforced sign of care.
- **Suggested change:** Keep. Use this voice as the model for fixing notes 4 and 5.
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep)

### 20. KEEP: The author note, with one optional sentence
- **Location:** Front matter, "A NOTE ON WHO WROTE THIS": "written by a trans man who spent more than ten years leading Bible studies… the one the handouts were about."
- **Reaction:** Seeing a trans author named on the first pages told me this book would probably be safe, and the folding-table image is lovely. Because the book then says almost nothing about gender, though, I finished wondering why the author kept that part of the story out of the pages meant for readers like me.
- **Suggested change:** Keep the note. If the author is comfortable, add one sentence such as: "Some of what he had to rebuild was what he'd been taught about gender, and that's part of why this book tries to make room for readers whose leaving and whose gender got tangled together." This only works if the body text delivers on it (notes 2 and 3).
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep)
