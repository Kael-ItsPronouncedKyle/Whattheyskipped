# Reader Review: Progressive / Liberal Ex-Evangelical Lens

*Reader profile: 33, ex-evangelical, now secular-ish with some spiritual practice, active in social justice spaces, has been in therapy, fluent in deconstruction-community language. Reviewed: all four manuscript files, front matter through Appendix I.*

## Overall verdict

This is a careful, kind workbook, and it is honest about its sources. I trust it more than most deconstruction material I've read. The practical-safety chapter, the crisis page with Trans Lifeline's consent policy, the warning about biblical counseling and the treatment of conversion therapy are better than anything I was handed when I left. But the book is so set on being "not anti-Christian" and not pushing anyone toward any outcome that it goes vague exactly where I needed it to be clear. Abuse gets one bullet point ("An abusive leader protected by the church"). Christian nationalism and the 2016–2020 political rupture, which is why a big share of my generation walked out, don't appear at all. Race doesn't appear at all. Survivors of sexual violence get no crisis line, no section and no acknowledgment in the purity-culture chapter. The "both" exercise and the parent-translation table risk teaching survivors to soften what happened to them. The implied reader is white, suburban, US-born, cisgender, non-disabled, raised in church from childhood and able to afford a climbing gym and a therapist. The book needs to say once, plainly, that it is not neutral about harm. It should then add short sections for survivors, for readers of color and immigrants, and for readers who left over politics. The language is mostly current and non-ableist; the problem is what the book leaves out, not how it phrases things. Fix the MUST FIX items below and this becomes a book I'd post in my group chats without caveats.

## Notes

### 1. The book never says it takes sides on abuse
- **Location:** Before You Begin, "What This Workbook Is Not"; also 1.3
- **Quote:** "It is not anti-Christian. A lot of what follows was written with gratitude for the good things many people found in church: the music, the casseroles, the people who showed up at the hospital."
- **Reaction:** I don't need the book to be anti-Christian. I do need to know it's on my side if I was abused. Right now the warmest paragraph in the front matter is about casseroles, and the only mention of abuse in the whole book is half a bullet in 1.3. The author's own method ("you don't blame the person standing closest to the problem. You trace the chain") is a perfect frame for institutional abuse, and the book never uses it that way.
- **Suggested change:** Add a fifth item after "It is not anti-Christian":
  > **It is not neutral about harm.** Being fair to people who still believe is not the same as being neutral about abuse. If someone in your church hurt you (sexually, physically, emotionally or spiritually), or if leaders protected the person who did, that was wrong. It was wrong whatever anyone believes about God, and nothing in this book asks you to see "both sides" of it.
  
  Then add a new **section 3.5, "If You Were Abused,"** of about 250 words. It should: (a) name clergy and church-volunteer sexual abuse, spiritual abuse, and institutional cover-up as patterns, not rare exceptions. Cite the Houston Chronicle / San Antonio Express-News "Abuse of Faith" series (2019) and the Guidepost Solutions report to the SBC Executive Committee (May 2022) [VERIFY figures before print]. (b) Say the reader doesn't have to confront, forgive or report on anyone's timeline but their own. (c) Point to RAINN and the National DV Hotline (see note 2). (d) Note that statutes of limitations vary and some states have opened "look-back windows," so a lawyer consult may be worth it.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 2. The crisis page has no line for sexual or domestic violence or child abuse
- **Location:** "If You Need Help Right Now" and Appendix A
- **Quote:** "If you are thinking about ending your life, or you are not safe where you are tonight, stop reading and contact one of these."
- **Reaction:** "Not safe where you are tonight" is in the opening line, but every number listed is a suicide or LGBTQ+ line. A woman whose husband just took the car keys because she stopped going to church has nowhere on this page to call.
- **Suggested change:** Add to both the crisis page and the Appendix A table:
  - **National Domestic Violence Hotline (US):** call **1-800-799-7233** or text **START** to **88788**. Includes help with financial and spiritual abuse.
  - **RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline (US):** call **1-800-656-4673** or chat at rainn.org.
  - **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (US):** call or text **1-800-422-4453**.
  - **StrongHearts Native Helpline (Native American and Alaska Native survivors):** **1-844-762-8483**.
  
  Re-check every number on the day of final layout, as with the existing ones.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. Purity culture without survivors and without politics
- **Location:** Chapter 8, 8.1–8.2
- **Quote:** "the idea that sexual purity was a gift you'd give your future spouse, and that losing it made you 'used'"
- **Reaction:** The most vicious thing purity culture did was tell kids who were raped that they were now "used." The chapter lists that idea and walks past it. It also frames purity culture as a church fad of the '90s, when the same ideas were backed by federal abstinence-only funding and tied to anti-abortion and anti-LGBTQ politics. Treating it as only a personal, interior issue makes it look smaller than it was.
- **Suggested change:** After the bullet list in 8.1, add:
  > Purity culture wasn't only a church program. From 1996 on, the US government funded abstinence-only-until-marriage education in public schools, much of it built on the same messages [VERIFY and cite: Social Security Act Title V §510; SIECUS funding history]. If you went to public school and still heard that sex before marriage would ruin you, that's why.
  
  In 8.2, add a short paragraph:
  > **If you were sexually assaulted.** Purity teaching was especially cruel to survivors. Elizabeth Smart, kidnapped and raped at fourteen, has said that a teacher's lesson comparing a girl who'd had sex to chewed gum made her feel worthless and that no one would want her [cite her 2013 Johns Hopkins remarks; VERIFY]. Many survivors were blamed for "causing" what was done to them, or told to forgive and keep quiet. None of that was true. What happened was done *to* you. If this is your story, a trauma-informed therapist matters more than any workbook, and RAINN (Appendix A) is free and confidential.
  
  Add Emily Joy Allison, *#ChurchToo* (Broadleaf, 2021) to the sources and to Further Reading.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 4. Christian nationalism and politics are missing
- **Location:** 1.3 "How It Usually Starts"; whole book (the word "politics" appears once, in 10.2)
- **Quote:** "**A betrayal.** An abusive leader protected by the church. A cover-up. A financial scandal."
- **Reaction:** For a lot of people my age, the betrayal was watching our churches respond to 2016, Charlottesville, George Floyd, COVID and January 6. The term the book defines in 1.1, "exvangelical," was coined that same year and is hard to separate from that political moment. A book about the first 90 days out that never mentions politics will read to me as either naive or carefully avoiding the topic, and I'll wonder which.
- **Suggested change:** Add a bullet to 1.3:
  > - **Politics.** Watching your church, or your parents, embrace a political movement, a candidate or a nationalism that seemed to contradict everything they'd taught you about loving your neighbor. For many people, the turning point was an election, a sermon about immigrants or protesters, or a church's response to a pandemic.
  
  Add a short section **6.4, "When Faith and Nation Were the Same Thing,"** of about 200 words. Define Christian nationalism as the belief that the US is or should be a Christian nation with laws privileging (conservative) Christianity. Cite Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, *Taking America Back for God* (Oxford, 2020), and PRRI/Brookings, "A Christian Nation?" (2023). Suggest which pile ("keep / return / unexamined") the reader puts it in. Keep it descriptive, not partisan: "You don't have to swap one political identity for another to notice this was part of what you were given." Add a Day prompt in Week 5, e.g. Day 34 alt: "What were you taught about America, the flag, or who counts as a 'real' American? Which pile does it go in?"
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 5. Race isn't mentioned anywhere
- **Location:** Whole book; most visibly 1.2, 9, 12, 14
- **Quote (1.2):** "It decided: who your friends were, and whose house you could sleep over at"
- **Reaction:** The word "race" never appears. Leaving looks very different if you're Black and your church is also your family's civil rights history, or Korean American and your church is where your parents speak their language, or Latina and leaving the church reads to your family as leaving the culture. It's also different if you left a white church because of how it treated you as a person of color. Every sources list is white or close to it. The imagery throughout (lock-ins, casseroles, climbing gyms) is suburban white megachurch.
- **Suggested change:** Add a section **9.6, "When Church Is Also Culture"**:
  > For many Black, Latino, Asian American, immigrant and Indigenous readers, church isn't only a religious community. It may be where your family's language is spoken, where your people organized for civil rights, or the one place your parents felt at home in a new country. Leaving can be heard as leaving your culture, your family's history or your community's survival. You don't have to choose between your faith questions and your people. Many people keep the food, music, language and family rituals while changing what they believe, and some find communities of people from the same background who've walked this road.
  > 
  > If you left a mostly white church partly because of how it treated race, or how it treated *you*, that's a legitimate reason, not "making it about politics."
  
  Add to 1.3 starting points: "A church's silence, or worse, about racism." Add to Further Reading: Jemar Tisby, *The Color of Compromise* (2019); Robert P. Jones, *White Too Long* (2020); Anthea Butler, *White Evangelical Racism* (2021); Candice Marie Benbow, *Red Lip Theology* (2022). Add Black Nonbelievers, Inc. to the Appendix A support list.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 6. The "both" exercise and "keep the person" can push survivors to soften what happened
- **Location:** 10.4 "Practicing 'Both'" and 6.3
- **Quote:** "'My church was all bad' becomes 'My church hurt me, *and* some people there were genuinely kind.'" / "You can keep the person and return the teaching. Your grandmother was not her worst idea."
- **Reaction:** This is good advice for a reader working through black-and-white thinking about their grandmother. It's harmful for a reader whose youth pastor groomed them. Being told to find the "and" is how many of us were pressured into forgiveness and silence in the first place. The exercise needs an explicit off-ramp.
- **Suggested change:** Add at the end of 10.4:
  > **One limit.** "Both" thinking is for complexity, not for abuse. If someone abused you, you don't need a balancing sentence about their good qualities. "He hurt me, and that was wrong" is a complete sentence. Noticing all-or-nothing thinking doesn't mean you owe anyone a kinder story than the true one.
  
  And in 6.3, after "You can keep the person and return the teaching," add: "You can also let the person go. Keeping someone is a choice, not an obligation, and it's never required of you when that person harmed you."
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 7. The parent table reads hostility as fear, and estrangement is never offered as an option
- **Location:** 9.1 and 9.2
- **Quote:** "Often it's fear wearing the costume of anger." / "'Who has been influencing you?' | 'I can't believe you got here on your own.'"
- **Reaction:** Some of us have parents who threatened to cut off tuition, outed us to the church, or told us we weren't welcome at Christmas. Translating that into "they're just scared" puts the job of managing their feelings back on me. The book never mentions low-contact or no-contact as a legitimate choice, though it's one of the most common topics in ex-evangelical communities.
- **Suggested change:** Add a final row to the 9.2 table: "'You're welcome here as long as you don't...' | Sometimes fear. Sometimes conditional love. You get to decide whether the condition is one you can live with." After 9.1's second paragraph, add:
  > Some people, after trying, decide to go low-contact or no-contact with a parent or family member. That's not failing to love them and it's not "bitterness." It's a boundary, and people make that choice for good reasons. If you're considering it, a therapist can help you think it through.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. Online communities and anger are tone-policed, and "cut off people" blurs boundaries with cult behavior
- **Location:** 12.2 "Online communities" and 14.3
- **Quote:** "Watch for spaces where anger at religion becomes the only topic; they can keep you stuck." / "watch for any group, religious or not, that asks you to stop asking questions, cut off people who disagree"
- **Reaction:** Being told my anger is the thing to watch for is exactly what my church said. The FAQ handles anger well ("often appropriate"), so 12.2 should match it. 14.3 is fair, since activist and online spaces can be high-control too, but "cut off people who disagree" doesn't distinguish disagreement from harm, and someone will read it as "boundaries = cult."
- **Suggested change:** Replace the 12.2 sentence with: "Anger in these spaces is often healthy; many people need somewhere to say out loud what happened. The thing to watch for is a group where anger is *required*, where you're mocked for missing parts of church or for still believing something." In 14.3, change "cut off people who disagree" to "cut off people *just for disagreeing* (which is different from you choosing distance from someone who harmed you)".
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 9. Immigrants: church jobs can carry immigration status, and church can be the only community
- **Location:** 3.1 Dependency Checklist; 3.4
- **Quote:** "In the US, religious organizations have broad legal room to require employees to share their beliefs"
- **Reaction:** If you're a pastor's kid on a dependent visa, or a worship leader on an R-1 religious-worker visa, losing your church job can mean losing your legal status. That's the highest-stakes dependency in the book, and it isn't on the checklist. Undocumented readers may also fear any "call emergency services" advice.
- **Suggested change:** Add to the 3.1 checklist:
  > - **Immigration status.** Is your visa, green card application, or a family member's, tied to a church, ministry or religious employer (for example, an R-1 religious-worker visa or a sponsorship)? If so, talk to an immigration attorney *before* resigning or disclosing anything. Many legal-aid groups offer free or low-cost consults.
  
  In 3.4, add a line pointing back to this item.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. Disability, chronic illness and neurodivergence are absent
- **Location:** 1.3 ("Your own body") and 2.1
- **Quote:** "**Your own body.** Coming out, a divorce, an illness, depression that didn't lift when you prayed harder."
- **Reaction:** The book acknowledges depression that didn't lift with prayer, then never comes back to it. Disabled and chronically ill people got some of the worst of evangelicalism: being prayed over in public without consent, being told healing failed because of their faith or sin, ADHD or autism treated as rebellion, psychiatric meds discouraged. None of that is named.
- **Suggested change:** Add to 2.1 or as a box in Chapter 2:
  > **If you're disabled, chronically ill or neurodivergent,** you may carry a particular kind of harm: being prayed over without being asked, being told your healing depended on your faith, or having your ADHD, autism or mental illness treated as sin or rebellion. Your body was never evidence of your spiritual state. If you were discouraged from medication or treatment, it's worth revisiting that with a doctor now.
  
  Add Amy Kenny, *My Body Is Not a Prayer Request* (Brazos, 2022) to Further Reading, noting that it is written from within faith.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. Class: the advice assumes money, and doesn't name financial control in marriages
- **Location:** 12.2, 13.4, 3.1
- **Quote:** "A climbing gym, a choir, a running club, a board game night" / "If you have insurance, your provider directory lists in-network therapists."
- **Reaction:** Climbing gyms cost $90 a month. Many of us leaving are broke, on Medicaid, or stay-at-home moms in complementarian marriages with no income of our own. The checklist asks "Whose name is the car in?" but not "Do you have access to money?"
- **Suggested change:** In 12.2 change the examples to "A library group, a community garden, a choir, a pickup game, a mutual-aid or volunteer shift, a board game night." In 13.4 add: "If you're on Medicaid, it covers mental health care; call the number on your card. Federally qualified health centers offer care on a sliding scale. Open Path Collective lists reduced-fee therapists [VERIFY current pricing]." Add to 3.1: "**Money.** Do you have a bank account in your own name? Does a spouse or parent control access to money? If you've been out of paid work while raising kids, what would you need to get back in?"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 12. Corporal punishment and "discipline" teaching aren't mentioned
- **Location:** 9.4 Kids; 2.1; 6.2 "Return" pile
- **Quote (9.4):** "Protect them from fear-based teaching, whichever direction it comes from."
- **Reaction:** A huge share of ex-evangelicals were spanked or worse under James Dobson's *Dare to Discipline* (1970) or Michael and Debi Pearl's *To Train Up a Child* (1994). That shows up in our bodies and in how we parent. Meanwhile "whichever direction it comes from" is a both-sides move in the one place the book could have named a specific harm.
- **Suggested change:** Add to the 6.2 "Return" examples: "the idea that hitting children is godly discipline." In 9.4 add: "If you were raised on 'spare the rod' teaching, you may notice it in your own reflexes as a parent. That's common, and parenting support or therapy can help you break the pattern." Keep "whichever direction" but follow it with: "That includes threats of hell, and it includes mocking a child for believing."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. Further Reading leans heavily on Christian-insider voices
- **Location:** Appendix E and chapter sources
- **Quote:** "**Brian McLaren, *Faith After Doubt* (2021).** Written by a former pastor for people who want to keep some form of faith through doubt."
- **Reaction:** The annotated list is Klein, Boss, McLaren, Burns, Gregoire, Winell and the house guides. Gregoire and McLaren are both writing to keep people in faith, and Fowler and *Four Views on Hell* are insider texts too. None of the ex-evangelical writers I actually read is here, and neither is any book on politics, race or abuse.
- **Suggested change:** Add to Appendix E (annotate each; VERIFY publication details):
  - Chrissy Stroop and Lauren O'Neal, eds., *Empty the Pews: Stories of Leaving the Church* (2019). First-person essays by exvangelicals.
  - Kristin Kobes Du Mez, *Jesus and John Wayne* (2020). How evangelical masculinity and politics fused.
  - Robert P. Jones, *White Too Long* (2020), or Anthea Butler, *White Evangelical Racism* (2021).
  - Emily Joy Allison, *#ChurchToo* (2021). Purity culture and sexual abuse.
  - Laura E. Anderson, *When Religion Hurts You* (2023). A licensed therapist on religious trauma, clinically grounded.
  - Bradley Onishi, *Preparing for War* (2023). A former evangelical on Christian nationalism.
  - Tia Levings, *A Well-Trained Wife* (2024). Memoir of leaving an abusive patriarchal marriage.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 14. The faith-stage models quietly treat non-belief as a phase
- **Location:** 10.3; Day 59 area; Appendix E McLaren annotation
- **Quote:** "the 'perplexity' stage, where everything seems questionable, is a normal part of the journey rather than a failure... the season you're in is a season, not your final state."
- **Reaction:** McLaren's endpoint is "harmony," a mature faith. Fowler's top stage is also religious. Putting these models in the "thinking" chapter suggests that if I land as an atheist, I got stuck in stage 3. That cuts against the book's own promise in the front matter.
- **Suggested change:** Add after "These models are descriptions, not laws": "Both were written by Christians, and both describe faith maturing rather than faith ending. If you end up not believing, that isn't a stalled stage. It's one of the destinations in Chapter 14." Change "the season you're in is a season, not your final state" to "how you feel right now isn't necessarily how you'll always feel, wherever you end up."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 15. "Where People Land" leaves out ancestral traditions and Black and liberation traditions
- **Location:** 14.2
- **Quote:** "Some people find a home in Judaism (often through conversion, which takes time and study), Buddhism, Quakerism, Unitarian Universalism, or other traditions."
- **Reaction:** For many readers of color, "another tradition" means reclaiming something missionaries took from their family: Indigenous spiritual practice, Afro-diasporic traditions, ancestral Buddhist or Hindu practice. "A different kind of Christianity" also skips the Black church and liberation and womanist theology, which is where a lot of Black ex-evangelicals land. Also, "affirming" churches aren't automatically safe on race.
- **Suggested change:** Add a portrait:
  > **Returning to roots.** Some people, especially those whose families were converted by missionaries or whose ancestors' practices were suppressed, find meaning in reconnecting with Indigenous, African-diasporic, or ancestral traditions their families set aside. That often means learning from living communities, not from books alone.
  
  In "A different kind of Christianity," add "historically Black churches and traditions shaped by liberation or womanist theology." In 12.2 "Affirming" bullet, add "Ask too how they handle race and disability; 'affirming' on one issue doesn't guarantee safety on others."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 16. "Mom and Dad" assumes a heterosexual two-parent family
- **Location:** 9.4 and Appendix C "To your kid"
- **Quote:** "Be honest at their level: 'Mom and Dad believe different things about God, and that's okay.'" / "'Mom and I believe different things about God right now.'"
- **Reaction:** In a book written by a trans man and aimed at a lot of queer readers, defaulting to "Mom and Dad" stands out. It also leaves out single parents and co-parents after divorce, who are common in exactly this situation.
- **Suggested change:** 9.4: "'Your dad and I believe different things about God' (or 'your other parent and I', or 'Grandma and I'), 'and that's okay.'" Appendix C: "'Your [other parent] and I believe different things about God right now. That's okay. You can ask either of us anything.'"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 17. LGBTQ+ youth being kicked out deserves concrete resources, not "many cities have organizations"
- **Location:** 3.2
- **Quote:** "Many cities have organizations that help young people who've been forced out of their homes."
- **Reaction:** This is the most dangerous scenario in the book for its most vulnerable readers, and the advice is a vague gesture.
- **Suggested change:** Replace with: "If you're forced out or about to be, the **National Runaway Safeline** (1-800-786-2929, 1800runaway.org) can help you find shelter and plan next steps, whatever your age up to 21 [VERIFY age range]. Local LGBTQ+ community centers (see Appendix A) often know which youth shelters are safe for queer and trans young people. If you can, keep copies of your ID, birth certificate and Social Security card somewhere outside the house."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 18. The 988 police caveat is good; the "immediate danger" line needs the same care
- **Location:** Crisis page
- **Quote:** "**If you are in immediate danger:** call your local emergency number."
- **Reaction:** I really appreciated that the book told me 988 can involve emergency services and that Trans Lifeline won't without consent. That's the kind of honesty my communities look for. But for Black, trans, disabled or undocumented readers, "call 911" can itself be dangerous, and the page doesn't acknowledge that.
- **Suggested change:** Keep the existing 988 and Trans Lifeline disclosures. Add after the immediate-danger line: "If calling the police doesn't feel safe for you, a domestic violence hotline (above) can help with emergency shelter and safety planning without police, and some cities have non-police crisis response teams. Search your city name plus 'mobile crisis team.'"
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 19. "Exvangelical" is defined without its critical edge
- **Location:** 1.1 and Appendix B glossary
- **Quote (glossary):** "**Exvangelical.** A former evangelical."
- **Reaction:** The term came out of a community that was explicitly criticizing evangelicalism's politics and harm, and defining it as just "a former evangelical" strips that out. The glossary also lacks terms the reader will run into on day two of reading online: Christian nationalism, spiritual abuse, DARVO, #ChurchToo, spiritual bypassing.
- **Suggested change:** Glossary: "**Exvangelical.** A former evangelical. The term, popularized by Blake Chastain from 2016, is often used by people who are also critical of evangelicalism's political and cultural influence." Add entries: **Christian nationalism**; **Spiritual abuse** ("using religious authority, scripture or belief to control, shame or exploit someone"); **#ChurchToo** ("the 2017 movement in which survivors shared experiences of sexual abuse in churches"); **DARVO** ("Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender, a pattern described by psychologist Jennifer Freyd, often seen when abuse is reported").
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 20. The ethics chapter is Western-only and leaves out justice
- **Location:** 11.2 and 11.3
- **Quote:** "You know that hurting people for fun is wrong... You knew those things in church, and you know them now."
- **Reaction:** It's honest that the table is "Western traditions," but the reader is left with four white European men plus two women. Nothing addresses ethics at the level of systems: who gets harmed by a policy, not just by a person. And some of us were taught in church that cruel things (spanking, excluding gay people, deporting neighbors) were *right*, so "you knew those things" is too rosy.
- **Suggested change:** Add a row or a sentence after the table: "Other traditions offer rich alternatives: Confucian ethics of relationship, the Southern African idea of *ubuntu* ('I am because we are'), Buddhist ethics of non-harm. And many people today add a justice question: *Who is harmed by the way things are arranged, and what do I owe them?*" In 11.3 add: "Some churches also taught things as right that you now see as harmful. Untangling those is part of the work in 11.4."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 21. The daily prompts assume a childhood in church
- **Location:** Day 3, Day 41, Day 45
- **Quote:** "**Day 3.** Describe your Sunday mornings as a kid in as much sensory detail as you can"
- **Reaction:** Adult converts, people recruited in college ministries, and people who married in exist, and some of them were hurt the worst because they joined as vulnerable adults. These prompts quietly leave them out.
- **Suggested change:** Day 3: "Describe your Sunday mornings in church (as a kid, or whenever you started going) in as much sensory detail as you can." Day 45: "What were you taught about your body, at whatever age you first heard it?"
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 22. The ritual list needs a trauma-informed caveat
- **Location:** 7.3
- **Quote:** "go to one last service on purpose, sit in the back, and leave before the end"
- **Reaction:** For someone whose abuser still preaches or volunteers there, this is not a ritual. Most readers will see that, but the book should say it.
- **Suggested change:** Add at the end of 7.3: "If the building or the people in it are connected to someone who harmed you, choose a ritual that doesn't put you back in the room. You don't owe that place a goodbye in person."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

## What works and should be kept

### 23. KEEP: The crisis page comes first, with honest disclosures
- **Location:** "If You Need Help Right Now"
- **Quote:** "It does not call emergency services without your consent." / "988 can, in rare cases of immediate danger, involve emergency services."
- **Reaction:** Putting the crisis page first, including Trans Lifeline, and being honest about police involvement is exactly right. Most resources skip this, and people in my communities notice when they do. "You do not have to be 'in crisis enough' to call" is the right tone.
- **Suggested change:** None beyond notes 2 and 18. Keep it on page one.
- **Severity:** KEEP

### 24. KEEP: Chapter 3, Practical Safety, is the best chapter in the book
- **Location:** Chapter 3, especially 3.1, 3.2, 3.3
- **Quote:** "it's easier to be honest when your rent isn't riding on someone's reaction." / "That isn't cowardice. It's planning." / "Privacy isn't the same as shame."
- **Reaction:** This is materialist in the best sense: it puts housing, insurance, phone plans and custody ahead of theology. It doesn't moralize about coming out "authentically." This is the chapter I'd screenshot and send to someone.
- **Suggested change:** Keep it as is, and strengthen it with the immigration and money items in notes 9 and 11.
- **Severity:** KEEP

### 25. KEEP: An honest, non-hyped treatment of "religious trauma"
- **Location:** 2.2
- **Quote:** "the study was funded, carried out, and published by the research center its authors belong to, and other researchers have questioned how its sample was drawn. Treat the number as a sign that you're far from alone, not as a settled fact."
- **Reaction:** I've seen the "1 in 3" stat passed around online with no caveats. It's refreshing, and builds trust, that this book takes the experience seriously while being honest about the evidence. It validates without overclaiming.
- **Suggested change:** None.
- **Severity:** KEEP

### 26. KEEP: Clear warnings about biblical counseling and conversion therapy
- **Location:** 13.2, 13.3, 13.5
- **Quote:** "If you're leaving a faith, a counselor whose goal is to bring you back to it is not neutral help." / "The ruling was about free speech. It did not find conversion therapy safe or effective."
- **Reaction:** This is concrete, protective and appropriately pointed. The credential table and the screening questions (especially "How do you handle it if a client's goals conflict with your own beliefs?") are genuinely useful. Naming "reintegrative therapy" as a rebrand is exactly what queer readers need.
- **Suggested change:** Add one screening question: "Do you have experience with clients of my background (race, culture, disability, gender identity)?"
- **Severity:** KEEP

### 27. KEEP: The marriage-safety line and the anger FAQ
