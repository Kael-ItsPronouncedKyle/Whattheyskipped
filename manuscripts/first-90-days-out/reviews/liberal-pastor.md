# Reader Review: *First 90 Days Out*, read by a progressive mainline pastor

**Reader persona:** An ordained mainline pastor (Episcopal priest / ELCA or UCC pastor). My congregation is LGBTQ-affirming, and every year it takes in several people coming out of evangelical churches. I read the full manuscript (front matter, Parts One to Three, Appendices A to I) and did not edit it.

---

## Overall verdict

This is a careful, humane workbook. I would hand it to people in my newcomers' class, and I would keep a stack in my office. It is good on the body, on grief, and on the hell fear. It is honest about religious counselors without being contemptuous. The opening promise that "this workbook doesn't care where you end up" is sincere, and the FAQ line "Coming back on purpose, after looking hard at it, is different from never having looked" is pastorally exact. But the book doesn't keep that promise all the way through. Once Part Three starts building a life, the default reader has left church and God. Staying Christian gets one bullet in Chapter 12, one paragraph in Chapter 14, and a glossary entry with a factual mistake in it. So it reads less as a real option and more as a polite footnote. The more serious problems are pastoral rather than tonal. Chapter 14 lists Catholicism, Orthodoxy, and "progressive evangelical communities" alongside affirming mainline churches without telling an LGBTQ+ reader (and this book's readership is heavily LGBTQ+) that these traditions' official teachings are not affirming. Chapter 13, "Choosing Help," treats religious helpers only as a risk and never mentions spiritual directors or affirming clergy, who are often the first and cheapest help a person in this transition can get. The book also says almost nothing about the actual evangelical-to-mainline path: churches that are affirming in name only, liturgy as a new language, the loss of evangelical intensity, and well-meaning progressive churches that rush newcomers into membership or volunteer work. None of this needs a new chapter. About three pages of targeted additions would make the book's neutrality true in practice.

## Notes

### 1. KEEP: The neutral framing in the front matter and the FAQ
- **Location:** Before You Begin, "A Note on Where You Are" / "What This Workbook Is Not"; Ch. 1.1; Appendix D
- **Quotes:** "This workbook doesn't care where you end up." / "Plenty of people deconstruct and remain Christian." / "Coming back on purpose, after looking hard at it, is different from never having looked."
- **Reaction:** This is the best possible opening for someone in my pews who isn't sure they belong in any pew. The book doesn't treat staying as failure, and it doesn't treat leaving as the brave choice. The FAQ line about coming back is something I'd say from a pulpit.
- **Suggested change:** Keep all three as written. Use them as the standard for the rest of the book (see Note 2).
- **Severity:** KEEP (no change)

### 2. Part Three quietly assumes the reader has left church, and God
- **Location:** Ch. 12 epigraph; 12.3; Ch. 11.1; Workbook Day 36, Day 66
- **Quotes:** "Church did one thing really well. You'll have to build it on purpose now." / "Church gave you those hours automatically. Now you have to choose them." / Day 36: "How could you keep it without the church?" / Day 66: "Someone asks, 'Without God, how do you know what's right?' Write your honest answer."
- **Reaction:** Each line is reasonable by itself, but together they make leaving the default and staying the exception. A reader who has spent sixty days discovering she wants to be an Episcopalian reaches Day 66 and is asked to defend ethics without God. She'll conclude the book wasn't written for her, which breaks the promise in the front matter.
- **Suggested change:**
  - Ch. 12 epigraph → "*Church did one thing really well. Whether or not you go back to one, you'll have to build it on purpose now.*"
  - 12.3, replace the last two sentences → "Your old church gave you those hours automatically. Wherever you find community next, a new congregation included, you'll have to choose them. That's why 'join something weekly' is more useful advice than 'make friends.'"
  - Day 36 → "What's one good habit church gave you? How could you keep it, in a different church or without one?"
  - Day 66 → "Someone asks, 'How do you know what's right now?' (If you still believe in God, they may ask, 'How do you know your new church isn't just telling you what you want to hear?') Write your honest answer."
  - 11.1, after "what stops me from becoming a terrible person?", add: "People moving to a different kind of church feel a version of this too: *If I stop reading the Bible the way I was taught, am I just picking the parts I like?*"
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. Chapter 14 lists non-affirming traditions with affirming ones and gives LGBTQ+ readers no warning
- **Location:** Ch. 14.2, "A different kind of Christianity"
- **Quote:** "They move to mainline Protestant churches (Episcopal, Lutheran ELCA, Presbyterian PC(USA), United Church of Christ, and others), to Catholicism or Orthodoxy, or to progressive evangelical communities."
- **Reaction:** For a straight cisgender reader this is a fair list. For the gay or trans reader this book clearly expects (Trevor Project, Trans Lifeline, Chapter 13.5), it sends them toward the Catholic and Orthodox churches with no mention that both officially teach against same-sex marriage and gender transition. "Progressive evangelical" is also where I see the most churches that are affirming in name only. This is a safety issue, not a matter of taste.
- **Suggested change:** Replace the paragraph with:
  > **A different kind of Christianity.** Some people find that what they left wasn't Christianity itself but one strict version of it. Common destinations include mainline Protestant churches (Episcopal, Lutheran ELCA, Presbyterian PC(USA), United Church of Christ, United Methodist, Disciples of Christ, and others), Catholicism or Orthodoxy, and progressive evangelical communities. These are not the same. The Episcopal Church, the ELCA, the PC(USA), the UCC, and, since 2024, the United Methodist Church all allow same-sex marriage and LGBTQ+ clergy at the denominational level, though individual congregations and clergy still vary. The Catholic and Orthodox churches officially teach that marriage is between a man and a woman and do not support gender transition, even where a local parish is warm. Many people are drawn to them by their liturgy, history, and seriousness, and some LGBTQ+ Catholics and Orthodox Christians stay and push for change. Go in with your eyes open. "Progressive evangelical" covers everything from fully affirming to "welcoming but not affirming," so ask (see Chapter 12). People in all of these churches hold the Bible as sacred. The mainline and Catholic traditions read it with long-standing tools (history, tradition, reason, the church's shared reading) rather than the "plain reading" you may have been taught.
- Add to Sources · Chapter 14: Episcopal General Convention 2015 (marriage rites) and 2018 (Resolution B012); ELCA Churchwide Assembly 2009; PC(USA) Amendment 10-A (2011) and Amendment 14-F (2015); UCC General Synod 2005; United Methodist General Conference 2024; *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §§2357–2359. CHECK EACH BEFORE PRINT.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 4. "Choosing Help" leaves out clergy and spiritual direction
- **Location:** Ch. 13.2 and 13.4; Appendix A, "If you're not in crisis but need support"
- **Quote:** "**'Christian counseling'** can mean a licensed therapist who is also a Christian … **'Biblical counseling'** … usually means something different"
- **Reaction:** Religious help appears in this chapter only as something to screen out. Yet the readers most likely to stay Christian are the ones most likely to need someone to talk to about God, not just about anxiety. An affirming pastor or a trained spiritual director is often free or cheap and available this week. Not mentioning us at all is a real gap. I'd also want readers warned that clergy are not therapists, and that spiritual direction is an unregulated field.
- **Suggested change:** Add a new section 13.3a before "Questions to ask a therapist":
  > ### SPIRITUAL DIRECTORS AND CLERGY
  > If you're still asking questions about God, and not only about your mood, two other kinds of help exist alongside therapy, not instead of it.
  >
  > **Spiritual direction** is an old practice of meeting regularly, often monthly, with a trained companion who helps you notice where you sense God, or don't, without telling you what to believe. Good directors don't steer you toward an outcome. Spiritual Directors International (sdicompanions.org) has a searchable directory. Spiritual direction isn't licensed, so ask: "Where did you train? Do you receive supervision? Have you worked with people leaving evangelical or high-control churches? Are you comfortable if I end up not believing?"
  >
  > **Clergy in affirming congregations** (Chapter 12) will often meet with someone who isn't a member, at no cost, just to listen. Many mainline clergy have done supervised hospital chaplaincy training as part of their preparation. They aren't therapists, and a good one will refer you to a therapist when you need one. You can say on the phone: "I'm coming out of an evangelical church and I'm not sure what I believe. I'm not ready to join anything. Would you be willing to talk?"
- Add both to Appendix A's support list.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 5. "Ask directly" is right, but readers need the actual questions to ask
- **Location:** Ch. 12.2, "Affirming or progressive congregations"
- **Quote:** "Ask directly about their stance before you invest; a church's website language can be vague."
- **Reaction:** This is correct, and it's the most important sentence in the book for my part of the world. But a newly ex-evangelical reader doesn't know what "directly" sounds like. "All are welcome" on a sign usually means "welcome to attend," not "welcome to marry, lead, or be ordained." The glossary already defines "affirming" well; Chapter 12 should use that definition.
- **Suggested change:** After that sentence, add:
  > "Welcoming" and "affirming" are not the same word. Questions that get a clear answer: *Will your pastor officiate a same-sex wedding here? Can LGBTQ+ people serve as clergy, elders, or teachers, and do they now? Is this written in a policy, or does it depend on who the pastor is? If I'm trans, will you use my name and pronouns, including in the baptismal or membership record?* A church that answers "we love everyone" without answering the question has answered it. Some denominations leave this to each congregation, so the name on the sign isn't enough.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 6. No directories of affirming churches
- **Location:** Ch. 12.2; Appendix A; Appendix E
- **Quote:** "many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people."
- **Reaction:** The book gives directories for crisis lines and therapists but none for the churches it mentions. Most mainline denominations run formal affirming-congregation programs, and these are the quickest way to find a church that has actually voted on the question.
- **Suggested change:** Add to Appendix A, under a new heading "If you want an affirming church":
  > Many denominations keep lists of congregations that have formally voted to be affirming: the UCC's **Open and Affirming** (openandaffirming.org), the ELCA's **Reconciling in Christ** (reconcilingworks.org), **More Light Presbyterians** (mlp.org), the United Methodist **Reconciling Ministries Network** (rmnetwork.org), and the Disciples of Christ's **Open and Affirming** program. **Church Clarity** (churchclarity.org) rates churches on how clearly they state their LGBTQ+ policies. **Q Christian Fellowship** (qchristian.org) supports LGBTQ+ Christians across traditions. CHECK EVERY URL AND WHETHER EACH PROGRAM IS STILL ACTIVE BEFORE PRINT.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 7. Nothing on liturgy as a new language
- **Location:** Ch. 12.2 / Ch. 14.2 (new sidebar)
- **Quote:** (no current text; 14.2 says only "They may hold the Bible as sacred while reading it differently.")
- **Reaction:** The first visit to a liturgical church throws former evangelicals more than any doctrine does: standing, kneeling, a prayer book, a confession of sin said aloud, a creed, a cup. Some find it a relief. For others, "we have sinned against you in thought, word, and deed" sets off the exact alarm Chapter 2 taught them to name. A half page of orientation would prevent a lot of first-visit walkouts.
- **Suggested change:** Add a sidebar at the end of 14.2:
  > **If you visit a liturgical church.** Episcopal, Lutheran, Catholic, Orthodox, and many Methodist and Presbyterian services follow a written order, often printed in a bulletin. You can follow along, sit when you're unsure, and stay silent during any words you can't say yet, including the creed and the prayer of confession. Clergy expect this, and many will tell you so. A spoken confession is meant to end with words of forgiveness, not more shame. If it sets off the old alarm, use your Chapter 2 sentence. Practices around Communion vary: some churches invite everyone, some invite the baptized, and you can always cross your arms for a blessing or stay in your seat. If you were baptized in an evangelical church with water in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, mainline churches will generally recognize that baptism. You won't be asked to be baptized again.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. The loss of evangelical intensity isn't named
- **Location:** Ch. 7.1 (list of losses); Ch. 14.2
- **Quote:** "singing with a room full of people"
- **Reaction:** The grief list comes close, but it doesn't name what my evangelical transplants say in month three: "It's beautiful here, but I don't *feel* anything." Mainline worship is quieter, congregations are often smaller and older, and there may be no young-adult group or weekly small group. People take the missing emotional high to mean God is missing, or they decide the problem is them. Naming this in advance keeps people from leaving a good church for the wrong reason.
- **Suggested change:** Add to 7.1's list: "the emotional intensity of worship: the band, the altar call, the feeling that God was *right there*." Then add to the 14.2 sidebar (Note 7): "Expect a different temperature. Mainline worship tends to be quieter, and many congregations are small and older. Some people find that restful. Others feel spiritually flat for months and assume something is wrong with them or with God. It's usually just a change of key. Give a new church at least a couple of months, and look for the people under fifty before you decide there aren't any."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 9. The risks inside progressive churches: rushing in, being put to work, contempt for where you came from
- **Location:** Ch. 14.3; Part One intro ("Nothing gets decided in Part One")
- **Quote:** "Wherever you land, watch for any group, religious or not, that asks you to stop asking questions, cut off people who disagree, or hand your judgment over to a leader."
- **Reaction:** 14.3 is good (see Note 19), but the risks I see in my own tradition are subtler than high control. A small, aging congregation that is thrilled to have a thirty-year-old ex-evangelical can have her on the vestry, telling her "testimony" at the stewardship dinner, and enrolled in confirmation within six weeks. Progressive churches can also be openly contemptuous of evangelicals, which reinforces the black-and-white thinking Chapter 10 warns about and shames people for loving their parents.
- **Suggested change:** Add to 14.3:
  > Two subtler things to watch for in a new church, even a good one. First, speed. A congregation that's glad to have you may ask you to join, be confirmed, serve on a committee, or tell your story within weeks. Your Part One rule still applies: you don't have to decide anything big in your first season, and a healthy church will wait. Second, contempt. If a new community talks about evangelicals the way your old church talked about "the world," notice it. You're allowed to leave a church without despising the people still in it.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. The glossary definition of "Mainline Protestant" is inaccurate and incomplete
- **Location:** Appendix B, Glossary
- **Quote:** "**Mainline Protestant.** Older, generally more progressive Protestant denominations in the US, such as the Episcopal Church, the ELCA, the PC(USA), and the United Church of Christ."
- **Reaction:** "Older" is wrong as a definition. The Southern Baptist Convention (1845) is older than the UCC (1957) or the ELCA (1988) as denominations, though the mainline bodies have older roots. "Mainline" is a historical and sociological term, not an age category. The list also leaves out the United Methodist Church, which is the mainline church most readers in small Southern and Midwestern towns will actually drive past. After roughly a quarter of US United Methodist congregations disaffiliated in 2019–2023, many of those now belong to the non-affirming Global Methodist Church, so a "Methodist" sign alone tells you little.
- **Suggested change:**
  > **Mainline Protestant.** A group of historically established US Protestant denominations, including the Episcopal Church, the ELCA, the PC(USA), the United Church of Christ, the United Methodist Church, the American Baptist Churches USA, and the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ). They are generally more theologically progressive than evangelical churches, and most now allow same-sex marriage and LGBTQ+ clergy, though congregations vary. Not every church with "Methodist," "Lutheran," or "Presbyterian" on the sign is mainline: the Global Methodist Church, the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, and the Presbyterian Church in America are separate, conservative denominations.
- Source: UMC General Council on Finance and Administration disaffiliation figures. CHECK PERCENTAGE BEFORE PRINT.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. Quakerism is placed outside Christianity
- **Location:** Ch. 12.2 ("Other traditions"); Ch. 14.2 ("Another tradition")
- **Quote:** "Some people find a home in Judaism … Buddhism, Quakerism, Unitarian Universalism, or other traditions."
- **Reaction:** The Religious Society of Friends began as a Christian movement, and most Friends worldwide are explicitly Christian. Many US "Friends churches" (Evangelical Friends Church International) have pastors, worship bands, and conservative sexual ethics, and would feel very familiar to an ex-evangelical. Liberal unprogrammed meetings range from Christian to nontheist. The book's label sends a reader looking for silence into something that may look exactly like what they left.
- **Suggested change:** In 14.2, move Quakers into the "different kind of Christianity" paragraph: "…or to Quaker meetings, which grew out of Christianity. Unprogrammed (silent) meetings tend to be theologically open and are often affirming; programmed 'Friends churches' can be quite evangelical, so ask." Keep Unitarian Universalism under "Another tradition," but add "(which grew out of Christian Unitarian and Universalist churches and is now non-creedal)." That parenthesis also connects nicely to the universalism point in 4.2.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 12. The hell first-aid facts leave out the most calming one for Christians who are staying
- **Location:** Ch. 4.2, Fact 1
- **Quote:** "Eternal punishment is still the official position of most large denominations; annihilation is a recognized minority view among evangelicals; universal reconciliation has prominent defenders but is officially rejected by the largest churches."
- **Reaction:** This is accurate as far as it goes, but it accepts the evangelical picture of what "official" hell means. Many churches that formally affirm hell define it as self-chosen separation from God, not God torturing people forever. The Episcopal catechism says, "By hell, we mean eternal death in our rejection of God," and the Catholic Catechism calls it a "state of definitive self-exclusion." Many mainline denominations say very little about it officially. For the reader staying Christian, "the church I'm visiting doesn't teach the hell I'm afraid of" is the fact that loosens the grip.
- **Suggested change:** Add after the existing sentence: "Even churches that officially affirm hell often describe it very differently from the fire-and-torment picture many evangelicals grew up with. The Episcopal Church's catechism says, 'By hell, we mean eternal death in our rejection of God'; the Catholic Catechism describes it as a 'state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God.' Many mainline Protestant churches say little about hell in their official teaching. If you visit one, you may not hear it preached at all." Sources: *The Book of Common Prayer* (1979), An Outline of the Faith, p. 862; *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1033.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. The only pastor script assumes the pastor is an adversary
- **Location:** Appendix C, "To a pastor who calls"
- **Quote:** "Thank you for checking on me. I'm taking some time away, and I'm not ready to talk about it."
- **Reaction:** That's a good script for the old pastor. But the reader who is shopping for a new church has no words for the most important call they'll make: the one to me. People sit in my parking lot for three Sundays because they don't know what to say.
- **Suggested change:** Add a script:
  > **To a pastor at a church you're thinking of visiting**
  > "I'm coming out of an evangelical church and I'm not sure what I believe right now. I'm not ready to join anything. Is that okay there? And can you tell me plainly where your church stands on LGBTQ+ people, including marriage and leadership?"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 14. Further Reading has only one book for readers staying Christian
- **Location:** Appendix E
- **Quote:** "**Brian McLaren, *Faith After Doubt* (2021).** Written by a former pastor for people who want to keep some form of faith through doubt."
- **Reaction:** McLaren is a good choice, but he's the only one. The memoir that a huge number of my ex-evangelical parishioners read, a former evangelical finding her way into the Episcopal Church, isn't here.
- **Suggested change:** Add:
  > - **Rachel Held Evans, *Searching for Sunday: Loving, Leaving, and Finding the Church* (Thomas Nelson, 2015).** An evangelical's account of leaving her church and eventually landing in the Episcopal Church, organized around the sacraments. Honest about what the move costs and what it gives.
  > - **Peter Enns, *The Sin of Certainty* (HarperOne, 2016).** A biblical scholar on why faith doesn't require certainty. Useful for readers who are keeping the Bible but losing the old way of reading it.
  > - **Nadia Bolz-Weber, *Pastrix* (Jericho Books, 2013).** A Lutheran (ELCA) pastor's memoir. Blunt, funny, and a good picture of what liturgy and grace can mean outside evangelicalism.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 15. The ritual list could include church rituals made for exactly this
- **Location:** Ch. 7.3, "Making Your Own Ritual"
- **Quote:** "Since nobody is going to hold a funeral for your old faith, you may want to make a small ritual of your own."
- **Reaction:** Some of us will hold one, with the reader, if asked. Mainline churches have rites for turning points: the ELCA's Affirmation of Baptism, the Episcopal reaffirmation of the Baptismal Covenant, and the lament psalms. A reader who is ending one kind of faith and not all faith may want a ritual that marks both an ending and a beginning.
- **Suggested change:** Add to the bullet list: "- read a psalm of lament aloud (Psalm 13 or Psalm 88), if the old words still mean something to you" and "- if you're finding your way into a different church, ask the pastor whether there's a rite for marking a new start. Many mainline churches have one (such as an affirmation or reaffirmation of baptism), and none of them requires you to have everything figured out."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 16. Holidays: name Blue Christmas and Longest Night services
- **Location:** Ch. 9.5; Ch. 15.2
- **Quote:** "Christmas and Easter can reopen the grief for years. Plan for them."
- **Reaction:** Many mainline churches hold a "Blue Christmas" or "Longest Night" service around December 21 for people who are grieving during the holidays. It's quiet, short, and asks nothing of you, which suits someone who isn't sure what they believe. A different church's Christmas Eve service can also let a reader keep the season without the old associations.
- **Suggested change:** Add to 9.5: "Some people find that a Christmas Eve or Easter service at a *different* kind of church lets them keep the music without the old weight. Many churches also hold a quiet 'Blue Christmas' or 'Longest Night' service in late December, meant for anyone who is grieving during the holidays, whatever they believe."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 17. Mixed-faith marriages include two Christians in different churches
- **Location:** Ch. 9.3, "Spouses and Partners"
- **Quote:** "If you're married to someone who still believes, you may be facing the hardest version of this."
- **Reaction:** A very common case in my office is two believers, where one spouse's move to an Episcopal or Lutheran church looks to the other like apostasy. The chapter frames it only as believer versus doubter.
- **Suggested change:** Add after the first paragraph: "This can be just as hard when you both still call yourselves Christian. If you're drawn to a mainline or affirming church, your spouse may experience that as leaving the faith, even though you don't. The same tools apply: talk about fears, not only beliefs."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 18. Small accuracy points in the hell word study
- **Location:** Ch. 4.2, Fact 2; Sources · Chapter 4
- **Quote:** "The King James Version used the single English word 'hell' for Gehenna, Hades, the Tartarus verse, and about half the occurrences of Sheol."
- **Reaction:** This is close, but the KJV translates *Hades* as "grave" once (1 Corinthians 15:55), so "for Hades" overstates it slightly. Also, the cited *Four Views on Hell* includes a fourth view, purgatory (Jerry Walls), which matters to readers heading toward Catholicism, while the text says "all three."
- **Suggested change:** "…for every occurrence of Gehenna, nearly every occurrence of Hades, the Tartarus verse, and about half the occurrences of Sheol." In Fact 1, optionally add: "(Catholic teaching adds purgatory, a state of purification after death for the saved, which is a separate question from hell.)"
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE
