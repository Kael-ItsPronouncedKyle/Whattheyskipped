# Reader Review: Theologically Conservative Evangelical Pastor

**Reader profile:** Reformed Baptist / PCA-type pastor, MDiv, holds to inerrancy and eternal judgment, does regular pastoral and biblical counseling, has walked with several members through deconstruction. A church member handed him this book. Pastoral, theologically precise, not hostile for its own sake.

**Manuscript:** *First 90 Days Out* (files 01–04). No manuscript files were edited.

---

## Overall verdict

This is a more careful and less hostile book than I expected to be handed, and I would rather a hurting member read this than most of what they'll find online at 2 a.m. The safety material, the crisis page, the honest caveats on "religious trauma" research, the charitable reading of frightened parents, and the warning that high-control habits can show up in any group are all things I'd say myself. My objections are less about what it says than what it leaves out. Over and over, the book describes conservative teaching through its worst practices: doubt treated as sin, "used goods" purity talk, blaming girls for boys' lust, fear of hell as the main motive. It never tells the reader that conservative theology itself rejects those things. Telling them would make the book more accurate, and it would help readers more, because it separates the theology from the abuse of it. Beyond that pattern, the book has two problems I'd call must-fix. It misdescribes biblical counseling, and the definition it gives isn't supported by its own cited source. And it says nothing about church membership and formal discipline, which is the most likely institutional consequence a member of a church like mine will actually face. Smaller fairness problems: the ethics chapter answers a weaker question than Christian thinkers actually ask; the hell "facts" suggest the doctrine depends on translation choices; and the "where people land" chapter and the reading list lean progressive, even though the book promises not to care where the reader ends up. Fix those and I'd disagree with the book's direction but couldn't call it unfair.

## Notes

### 1. Biblical counseling is defined inaccurately
- **Location:** Ch. 13, §13.2; repeated in Appendix B, "Biblical counseling"
- **Quote:** "counseling based on the view that Scripture alone is sufficient for emotional problems" / "(also called 'nouthetic counseling,' a movement launched by Jay Adams's 1970 book *Competent to Counsel*)" / "they may not be bound by the same professional and ethical rules"
- **Reaction:** This isn't how the movement describes itself, and the cited source (ACBC's certification FAQ) only supports the point about certification versus licensure, not the definition. Mainstream biblical counseling claims that Scripture is sufficient to address the spiritual and moral dimensions of "problems in living." It does not claim to treat medical conditions, and ACBC and CCEF counselors routinely refer people to physicians. Treating "nouthetic" as a synonym also flattens fifty years of history: CCEF (Powlison, Welch) deliberately broadened Adams's approach. ACBC also has a published code of conduct and a discipline process. That process isn't enforced by the state, but saying there's none is wrong.
- **Suggested change:** Replace the §13.2 paragraph with:
  > **"Biblical counseling"** is a movement that grew out of Jay Adams's 1970 book *Competent to Counsel* (Adams called his approach "nouthetic counseling"; later leaders such as David Powlison broadened it). Its central conviction is that Scripture is sufficient to address the spiritual and moral side of life's problems, and that counseling is a ministry of the church. Most biblical counselors say they will refer clients to a doctor for medical issues, though views on psychiatric diagnosis and medication vary. Many are certified by church-based organizations such as the Association of Certified Biblical Counselors rather than licensed by the state. Certifying bodies have their own codes of conduct, but those codes are not enforced by a state licensing board, and protections like confidentiality law and the formal complaint process may differ. Biblical counselors are generally open about the fact that their aim is to help you follow Christ. If you're leaving a faith, that is not neutral help.

  Glossary: "Biblical counseling. A church-based counseling movement holding that Scripture is sufficient to address the spiritual and moral side of life's problems; counselors are usually certified by religious organizations rather than licensed by the state (Chapter 13)." Add ACBC's Standards of Conduct and a CCEF or Powlison source (e.g., David Powlison, *The Biblical Counseling Movement: History and Context*, New Growth Press, 2010) to the Chapter 13 sources.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 2. Church membership and formal discipline are missing from "Practical Safety"
- **Location:** Ch. 3 (dependency checklist §3.1) and Ch. 5; Appendix C, "To a pastor who calls"
- **Quote:** "Before you make any announcements, look at what you depend on." (No mention of membership, membership covenants, or church discipline anywhere in the book.)
- **Reaction:** In a Reformed Baptist, PCA, or other church with formal membership, a member who stops attending or renounces the faith will very likely get elder visits, letters, and possibly formal discipline or removal from the rolls, sometimes announced to the congregation. I believe discipline, done rightly, is restorative (Matt. 18:15–17; Gal. 6:1). But a member who doesn't see it coming will experience it as an ambush. The book promises practical safety and leaves out the most predictable institutional consequence.
- **Suggested change:** Add §3.5:
  > ### 3.5 IF YOU'RE A CHURCH MEMBER
  > Many churches, especially Baptist, Presbyterian, and Reformed ones, have formal membership, and their elders have usually made vows to watch over members. If you stop attending or tell leaders you no longer believe, expect visits, calls, or letters. In some churches this can lead to a formal process called church discipline, which may end with your name being removed from the membership roll or announced to the congregation. Churches that practice discipline say its purpose is restoration, not punishment, and how it's carried out varies a lot. Practical steps: find and read your church's membership covenant, constitution, or book of order (for example, the PCA's *Book of Church Order*), so you know what process applies. In many churches you can ask in writing to resign or transfer your membership, though some denominations limit when a member may do so. If you want to avoid a formal process, a short, polite written request, sent before things become adversarial, often makes things simpler. Visits from elders are frequently a sense of duty, not surveillance, but you are still allowed to decline them.

  Add "Church membership: are you a formal member? Have you read the membership covenant?" to the §3.1 checklist.
- **Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. Hell, fact 1: the historic consensus is understated
- **Location:** Ch. 4, §4.2, fact 1
- **Quote:** "Eternal conscious punishment has been the majority view for most of church history"
- **Reaction:** "Majority view" is technically true but understates it. Eternal punishment is written into the major creeds and confessions (the Athanasian Creed; Augsburg Confession art. 17, which explicitly condemns those who teach an end to punishment; Westminster Confession 33; 1689 London Baptist Confession 32), and Origen's universalism was condemned in the sixth century. A reader who checks will feel handled.
- **Suggested change:** "Eternal conscious punishment has been the dominant view through nearly all of church history and is written into the major creeds and Reformation-era confessions (for example, the Athanasian Creed, the Augsburg Confession, and the Westminster Confession). Universal reconciliation was taught by some early figures such as Origen, and versions of it were condemned in the sixth century. Annihilation has had defenders in every era, and has gained more in the last century." Add the confessions to the Chapter 4 sources.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 4. Hell, fact 2: the word study implies the doctrine rests on translation
- **Location:** Ch. 4, §4.2, fact 2
- **Quote:** "The King James Version used the single English word 'hell' for Gehenna, Hades, the Tartarus verse, and about half the occurrences of Sheol. That blurred distinctions..."
- **Reaction:** The facts are mostly right, but placed in a list of things that "loosen the grip," they suggest the doctrine is a translation artifact. No serious defender of eternal punishment builds the case on the English word "hell"; we build it on Jesus' Gehenna sayings and texts like Matthew 25:46, Mark 9:43–48, and Revelation 14:11 and 20:10, which modern translations render just as starkly. Small precision points: the KJV renders *Hades* as "hell" in ten of its eleven occurrences (1 Cor. 15:55 has "grave"), and the English Revised Version (1881–85) restored the distinctions before the ASV did.
- **Suggested change:** Use "for Gehenna, nearly every occurrence of Hades, the Tartarus verse..." and "later translations such as the Revised Version (1881–85) and the American Standard Version (1901)." Add at the end: "That's a fact about translation, not a refutation of the doctrine. Defenders of eternal punishment rest their case on passages such as Matthew 25:46 and Mark 9:43–48, which say much the same thing in modern translations. If you want to weigh that case, do it in daylight, with the full arguments on all sides (see *Four Views on Hell*)."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 5. Sheol "where everyone goes" needs a hedge
- **Location:** Ch. 4, §4.2, fact 2
- **Quote:** "The Hebrew Bible's *Sheol* is the realm of the dead, where everyone goes, good or bad."
- **Reaction:** That's the common view, but it's stated more flatly than the scholarship supports. Philip Johnston (*Shades of Sheol*, IVP, 2002) notes that Sheol is invoked mostly for the wicked or for untimely and grievous deaths, and texts like Psalm 49:15 hint at hope beyond it. A 1906 lexicon is a thin source for a contested point.
- **Suggested change:** "The Hebrew Bible's *Sheol* is usually understood as the shadowy realm of the dead, where in many texts the righteous and the wicked alike go, though some scholars note it is mentioned mostly in connection with the wicked or with untimely death." Add Johnston, *Shades of Sheol*, to the sources.
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 6. Hell, fact 4: an appeal to popularity, and a figure to verify
- **Location:** Ch. 4, §4.2, fact 4
- **Quote:** "91 percent of American adults said they believe in God or a higher power, but only 62 percent said they believe in hell."
- **Reaction:** Fact 3 rightly says fear isn't evidence. By the same standard, a poll isn't evidence either. If the doctrine is true, a survey doesn't make it less true. Appendix H's own standards ("If only one source says something...") cut against using a headcount this way. Please also confirm that the 91 percent figure actually appears in the November 2021 "Views on the Afterlife" report and doesn't come from a different Pew survey.
- **Suggested change:** Add: "This doesn't tell you whether hell is real, any more than your fear does. It tells you that believing in God and believing in hell are separable questions for many people, so you can take them one at a time." Verify the source for 91 percent, or replace it with the figure the report actually gives.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 7. "Doubt is sin" is a distortion that conservative theology itself rejects. Say so
- **Location:** Ch. 6, §6.2 ("the idea that doubt is sin" in the Return pile); Ch. 11, §11.4 ("Whether doubt is a sin")
- **Quote:** "the idea that doubt is sin"
- **Reaction:** That's not what we teach. Scripture shows doubt being met with mercy ("Have mercy on those who doubt," Jude 22; "I believe; help my unbelief," Mark 9:24; Thomas in John 20), and the Westminster Confession 18.4 says true believers may have their assurance "shaken, diminished, and intermitted." A reader who was told doubting was itself rebellion deserves to know that their own tradition's confessions disagree. That frees them whether they stay or go.
- **Suggested change:** Add to §6.2, after the Return examples: "Some of what lands in 'return' was a distortion even by the standards of the tradition that taught it. Many conservative churches, for instance, officially teach that doubt is not the same as unbelief. The Westminster Confession says true believers can have their assurance 'shaken.' If you were told otherwise, the problem may have been the teaching you got, not only the theology behind it."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. "Package deal": interconnected doctrine isn't the same as control
- **Location:** Ch. 6, §6.1
- **Quote:** "High-control churches often teach faith as a package deal. If you doubt the creation account, you've doubted Jesus."
- **Reaction:** Some doctrines really do depend on each other. Jesus himself cites Genesis (Matt. 19:4–6), so asking how the two connect is a fair theological argument, not a manipulation tactic. What's harmful is using that argument to end the conversation. Christians have also long distinguished essentials from secondary matters (Paul in Romans 14; the old maxim "in essentials unity, in non-essentials liberty"), so even conservative churches don't treat every belief as load-bearing.
- **Suggested change:** Add after the first paragraph: "To be fair, some beliefs really are connected, and thoughtful Christians argue about how. That's a real argument you can weigh later. The harm comes when the connection is used to shut down a question instead of answering it. Many traditions also distinguish 'essentials' from secondary issues, so even within Christianity not every belief has been treated as load-bearing."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 9. Black-and-white thinking: a theological claim isn't a cognitive distortion
- **Location:** Ch. 10, §10.1–10.2
- **Quote:** "Many high-control churches teach in absolutes. Saved or lost. Heaven or hell. Truth or lies."
- **Reaction:** Several of those binaries come straight from Jesus' mouth (Matt. 7:13–14; John 3:36). Whether they're true is a theological question. "All-or-nothing thinking," in Beck's and Burns's sense, is something else: applying binaries where reality comes in shades, especially to yourself and other people. Blurring the two gives the impression that believing in salvation is itself a thinking error.
- **Suggested change:** Add to §10.2: "One distinction matters here. Some of those binaries are claims the Bible itself makes, and whether they're true is a theological question this chapter can't settle. All-or-nothing thinking, as therapists use the term, is the habit of forcing a two-bin answer onto things that come in shades: people, your past, your own worth. That's the habit worth noticing, whatever you end up believing about heaven and hell."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. Purity culture: separate the sexual ethic from the methods, and note conservative critics
- **Location:** Ch. 8, §8.1 and §8.3
- **Quote:** "the idea that sexual purity was a gift you'd give your future spouse, and that losing it made you 'used'" / "modesty teaching that held girls responsible for boys' thoughts" / "Your body isn't a moral test."
- **Reaction:** A pastor will concede most of the list. "Used goods" contradicts our own doctrine of forgiveness ("such were some of you. But you were washed," 1 Cor. 6:11), and Jesus put the responsibility for lust on the man who looks (Matt. 5:28). But the chapter never distinguishes the historic Christian sexual ethic (sex belongs within marriage), which many readers may still hold, from purity culture's shame-based methods and prosperity-style promises. Without that distinction, a reader who wants to keep the ethic and lose the shame has no place to stand.
- **Suggested change:** Add to §8.1 after the bullet list: "It helps to separate two things. One is a sexual ethic, the belief that sex belongs in marriage, which Christians have held for two thousand years and many still hold. The other is the set of methods purity culture used to teach it: shame, fear, object lessons about chewed gum and torn roses, and promises of a great marriage as a reward. Many pastors, including conservative ones, now criticize those methods as contradicting their own teaching about grace and forgiveness. You can decide about the ethic separately from the shame." In §8.3, change "Your body isn't a moral test" to "Your body isn't the enemy," which makes the point without seeming to rule on sexual ethics.
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. The complementarian definition omits the part its holders consider central
- **Location:** Appendix B, "Complementarian / egalitarian"
- **Quote:** "Complementarians hold that men and women have different God-given roles, with men leading in church and home."
- **Reaction:** A fair definition should be one its holders would sign. Complementarians (e.g., the Danvers Statement, 1988) insist on equal worth and dignity as part of the position, and the debate is mostly over what roles follow from it.
- **Suggested change:** "Complementarians hold that men and women are equal in worth but have different God-given roles, with men called to lead in the church and the home; views differ on how far this extends. Egalitarians hold that roles in church and home shouldn't be assigned by gender." Optionally cite the Danvers Statement (CBMW, 1988) and Christians for Biblical Equality's "Men, Women and Biblical Equality" (1989).
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 12. Ethics chapter: it answers a weaker version of the apologist's question
- **Location:** Ch. 11, §11.1 and §11.3
- **Quote:** "It also has a long and serious history of answers, and almost none of them involve becoming a terrible person." / "The rules didn't create those convictions. At best, they gave them a vocabulary."
- **Reaction:** I'd actually agree that unbelievers know right from wrong, and so does Paul: the law is "written on their hearts" (Rom. 2:14–15). But when Christian thinkers ask "Without God, how do you know what's right?", the serious version is usually about what *grounds* morality and makes it binding, not whether atheists can be decent. The chapter answers the easier question and leaves the reader unready for the one they'll actually hear.
- **Suggested change:** Add to §11.3: "Many Christians would agree with this. Paul says in Romans 2 that people without the biblical law still have a moral law 'written on their hearts.' When a thoughtful believer asks 'How do you know what's right?', they're often asking a different question: not whether you can be good, but what makes right and wrong *binding* rather than just preferences. That's a live debate in philosophy, with serious religious and non-religious answers. You don't have to settle it this month to keep your promises and defend the bullied kid." Optionally add a fifth table row: "**Natural law / divine command** | What fits the purpose built into human nature, or what has God commanded? | Thomas Aquinas; many Christian, Jewish, and Muslim thinkers."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. Counselor neutrality should run in both directions
- **Location:** Ch. 13, §13.2 (last sentence) and §13.4 (last bullet)
- **Quote:** "If you're leaving a faith, a counselor whose goal is to bring you back to it is not neutral help."
- **Reaction:** Fair, and I'd concede it. But the book's own question in §13.3 ("will you support either direction?") is better than this sentence, because it cuts both ways. A therapist who treats a client's remaining faith as a symptom to resolve isn't neutral either, and the book's promise not to care where the reader lands requires saying so.
- **Suggested change:** Add: "The same goes the other way: a therapist who treats any faith you still have as a symptom to be cured isn't neutral either. The question in 13.3 about supporting either direction is the one that matters."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 14. Conversion therapy section: give the reader the question that separates cases
- **Location:** Ch. 13, §13.5
- **Quote:** "sometimes under names that critics identify as rebrands, such as 'reintegrative therapy' or counseling for 'unwanted same-sex attraction.'"
- **Reaction:** I counsel same-sex-attracted members who come to me asking for help living by convictions they hold. I don't promise or pursue orientation change, and I'd object to calling that conversion therapy. The book attributes the "rebrand" claim to critics, which is fair, but the reader would be better served by a test than a label.
- **Suggested change:** Add: "Not every religious counselor who talks with an LGBTQ+ client about faith is practicing conversion therapy. The term refers to efforts aimed at changing sexual orientation or gender identity. Ask any counselor directly: 'Is changing my orientation or gender identity a goal of your work with me?' A clear answer to that question tells you more than the name on the door."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 15. The safety map and pastor script assume no believer is safe
- **Location:** Ch. 5, §5.2 table; Appendix C, "To a pastor who calls"
- **Quote:** "Safe now | ... | A friend who left church years ago; a non-religious coworker"
- **Reaction:** Every "Safe now" example is someone outside the faith, and the only pastor script is a polite deflection. Some believers, and some pastors, can listen without fixing. A reader who is still attending (the book explicitly welcomes them) gets no script for wanting to talk to one.
- **Suggested change:** In the table, add to "Safe now": "a believer who has shown they can listen without pushing." In Appendix C, add: "**To a pastor, if you do want to talk:** 'I'd like to talk, but I need you to listen first and not try to answer everything in one conversation. Can we start there?'"
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 16. Kids in a mixed-faith home: co-parenting, and what 1 Corinthians 7 actually says
- **Location:** Ch. 9, §9.3–9.4
- **Quote:** "Protect them from fear-based teaching, whichever direction it comes from."
- **Reaction:** I agree with the principle, but a reader could take this as license to pull the children out of church on their own, which can wreck a marriage and, as Chapter 3 notes, can matter legally under a parenting agreement. It's also worth telling the reader something reassuring that's true: Paul instructs a believer to stay with an unbelieving spouse who is willing to stay (1 Cor. 7:12–14), and many conservative churches teach exactly that.
- **Suggested change:** In §9.4, add: "Decisions about church attendance and religious teaching are usually best made with the other parent, not around them. If you share custody, check any parenting agreement first (see Chapter 3)." In §9.3, add: "If your spouse's church is theologically conservative, it may surprise you that many such churches teach, following 1 Corinthians 7:12–14, that a believer should stay married to a spouse who no longer believes, if that spouse is willing to stay."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 17. Doubter-friendly communities aren't only progressive ones
- **Location:** Ch. 12, §12.2
- **Quote:** "Affirming or progressive congregations. For people who want to keep some form of faith practice, many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people."
- **Reaction:** That's not the whole picture. Many theologically conservative churches welcome doubters and hold honest-questions classes. Implying that only progressive churches welcome questions is unfair, and it shuts out a reader who wants to stay orthodox but leave a harmful congregation.
- **Suggested change:** Split into two bullets. "**Affirming or progressive congregations.** Many denominations and individual churches fully include LGBTQ+ people; ask directly about their stance before you invest, since website language can be vague." and "**A different church in a similar tradition.** Some people find that what hurt them was one congregation or leader, not their whole tradition. Some theologically conservative churches make real room for doubt and questions. Ask how they've handled members who doubt, and how leaders are held accountable."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 18. "Where people land" leaves out a common destination
- **Location:** Ch. 14, §14.2, "A different kind of Christianity"
- **Quote:** "They move to mainline Protestant churches (...), to Catholicism or Orthodoxy, or to progressive evangelical communities."
- **Reaction:** A real and common landing spot is missing: people who leave a revivalist, fundamentalist, or abusive church for a liturgical or confessional church that is still theologically conservative (Anglican, confessional Lutheran or Reformed, and so on). Leaving them out tilts a chapter that promises "no ranking."
- **Suggested change:** Add after the first sentence: "For some, that means a church that is still theologically conservative but very different in culture: more liturgical, more confessional, or simply healthier in how it uses authority. Others move to mainline Protestant churches..."
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 19. The reading list should include orthodox voices on doubt and abuse
- **Location:** Appendix E
- **Quote:** "**Brian McLaren, *Faith After Doubt* (2021).** Written by a former pastor for people who want to keep some form of faith through doubt."
- **Reaction:** The only resource for keeping faith is from a leading progressive. For a reader who wants to stay orthodox, there are good books by evangelicals who take doubt and spiritual abuse seriously, and they'd make the book's "we don't care where you land" promise credible.
- **Suggested change:** Add: "**Os Guinness, *God in the Dark* (Crossway, 1996).** A short, sympathetic look at doubt from within historic Christian faith." "**Diane Langberg, *Redeeming Power* (Brazos, 2020).** A Christian psychologist on abuse of power in churches." "**Wade Mullen, *Something's Not Right* (Tyndale, 2020).** On the tactics abusive leaders and institutions use, by an evangelical writer." (Verify editions before print.)
- **Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 20. The Rapture fear is one tradition's, not all evangelicals'
- **Location:** Ch. 2, §2.1
- **Quote:** "or of the Rapture having happened while they slept"
- **Reaction:** That's not what we teach. Reformed, Presbyterian, and many Baptist churches don't hold a pre-tribulation Rapture. It's a real fear for people from dispensational churches, and naming its source helps readers see that "evangelical" isn't one thing.
- **Suggested change:** "or, for people raised in churches that taught a secret pre-tribulation Rapture, of it having happened while they slept"
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 21. Small uncharitable asides
- **Location:** Ch. 1, §1.3; Appendix I
- **Quote:** "a coworker from another religion who was obviously kinder than the elders" / "the 'clobber passages'" / "the seven passages used against LGBTQ+ Christians"
- **Reaction:** "Kinder than the elders" is a jab at a whole office; plenty of elders are kind. "Clobber passages" is an advocacy label, and "used against" assumes the conclusion. Each is small, but they undercut the front matter's "not anti-Christian" promise.
- **Suggested change:** "obviously kinder than some of the Christians you knew"; "the so-called 'clobber passages' (the verses traditionally read as prohibiting same-sex relationships)"; Appendix I: "the seven passages traditionally read as addressing same-sex relationships."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 22. The RSV 1946 claim needs one more clause
- **Location:** Appendix I, *Unbound* description
- **Quote:** "the 1946 Revised Standard Version, which brought the word 'homosexuals' into a major English Bible"
- **Reaction:** That's accurate as far as it goes, but readers may infer that the church's reading of 1 Corinthians 6:9 began in 1946. The KJV's "abusers of themselves with mankind" and the church's historic interpretation are much older. Whether that reading is right is what *Unbound* argues; this ad shouldn't imply the question is settled.
- **Suggested change:** "...which first used the word 'homosexuals' (in 1 Corinthians 6:9) in a major English Bible, a translation choice still debated today."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 23. The glossary drops Chapter 2's careful wording on religious trauma
- **Location:** Appendix B, "Religious trauma"
- **Quote:** "Lasting harm from religious beliefs, practices, or communities."
- **Reaction:** Chapter 2 carefully says the term "describes effects, not theology." The glossary says harm comes "from religious beliefs," which is the overreach the chapter avoided.
- **Suggested change:** "Lasting harm a person experiences in connection with religious beliefs, practices, or communities. It describes an effect on a person, not a verdict on a religion. Not a formal diagnosis (Chapter 2)."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 24. KEEP: the critique of fear-based teaching and the 2 a.m. advice
- **Location:** Ch. 4, §4.1 and §4.3; Ch. 2, §2.3; Ch. 6, §6.2 ("fear of hell as a motivator")
- **Quote:** "Go physical before you go theological... Argue with the theology in daylight." / "The belief left; the fear stayed."
- **Reaction:** I'd concede this entirely. Scaring children into professions of faith is something many Reformed pastors have preached against; our own tradition says "perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18) and puts gratitude, not terror, at the root of obedience (the Heidelberg Catechism's guilt, grace, gratitude). Telling someone not to wrestle doctrine while panicking at 2 a.m. is sound pastoral advice whatever they conclude.
- **Suggested change:** Keep as is. Optional addition to §4.1: "Many Christian teachers, including conservative ones, also reject using fear of hell to pressure children. The Bible itself says 'perfect love casts out fear' (1 John 4:18)."
- **Severity:** NICE TO HAVE (keep)
