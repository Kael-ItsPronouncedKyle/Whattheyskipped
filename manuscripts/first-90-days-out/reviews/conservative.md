# Sensitivity Read: Conservative / Evangelical Lay Reader

**Manuscript:** *First 90 Days Out* (files 01–04)
**Reader persona:** A 45-year-old evangelical parent whose adult child is deconstructing, who picked this up to understand what their child is going through. Also read with a second persona in mind: a conservative person who is questioning but still values tradition, family, and faith.
**Test applied:** The book's own claim, "This workbook doesn't care where you end up," plus "Every claim sourced."

---

## Overall verdict

This book is kinder and more careful than I expected. I picked it up ready for a hit piece, and it isn't one. The author tells me where he stands. He hedges the religious-trauma statistics honestly. He admits that eternal punishment has been the majority Christian view. He tells my child to be patient with me, and he warns against angry ex-religion spaces as well as controlling churches. Even so, the claim that it "doesn't care where you end up" does not hold up from where I sit. The book cares about where people end up in small, steady ways, and I can find each one. The examples it gives of "safe" people are non-religious or progressive. Its examples of "unsafe" people are devout. The "facts that help" all point away from traditional belief. Chapter 10 treats real truth claims (did Jesus rise or not?) as if they were a thinking error. Chapter 8 makes no room for someone who drops purity culture's shame but keeps the historic Christian sexual ethic. Chapter 14 has a portrait for every outcome except staying in, or coming back to, orthodox evangelical faith. And there is not one orthodox title in the reading list. None of this is hostile, and that is why it matters: a careful reader will notice it and stop trusting the neutrality claim. About seven targeted fixes would earn real trust from a reader like me. Most are a sentence or two each. One is a short new portrait in Chapter 14. One is a few titles in Appendix E. The book could then honestly say what it already wants to say.

## Notes

### 1. Front matter, "What This Workbook Is Not": the "manual for staying" paragraph
**Quote:** "If you came hoping someone would talk you back into certainty, this isn't that book. There are many of those, and you have probably read some of them already."
**Reaction:** This is the first place the neutrality promise slips. It treats staying in faith as wanting to be "talked back into certainty," as if nobody stays for good reasons, and the "you have probably read some of them already" line is a small eye-roll at the books I gave my kid.
**Suggested change:** Replace with: "It is not a manual for staying, either. This book won't argue you into or out of faith. If you want serious books that make the case for Christian belief, Appendix E lists some alongside books by people who left."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 2. Chapter 5.2, "The Safety Map" table
**Quote:** "| **Safe now** | Will listen without trying to fix you or win you back | A friend who left church years ago; a non-religious coworker |" … "| **Not yet** | … | Parents in leadership; a spouse still deeply invested |"
**Reaction:** The definitions are about behavior, and that's fair. But every "safe" example is non-religious or progressive ("a progressive aunt"), and every "not yet" example is devout. "A boss at a Christian school" also sits in the same box as "an abusive ex." The examples teach my child that how much faith someone has tells you whether they're safe, which is exactly the all-or-nothing thinking Chapter 10 warns against.
**Suggested change:** Make the examples mixed on purpose. Safe now: "A friend who left church years ago; a believing friend who has always let you think out loud; a non-religious coworker." Safe later: "A sibling; an aunt who's been through her own hard questions." Not yet: "A parent who reacts to fear by lecturing; an ex-churchmate who likes to post about people who leave." Maybe never: "Anyone who has used your secrets against you before; an employer whose contract makes disclosure risky." Add one sentence under the table: "Belief isn't the test. Plenty of devout people are excellent listeners, and plenty of non-religious people aren't."
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 3. "High-control" used as a stand-in for evangelical (2.1, 6.1, 10.1, and throughout)
**Quote:** "High-control churches often teach faith as a package deal." / "Many high-control churches teach in absolutes." / "after leaving a high-control faith"
**Reaction:** The book's audience is evangelicals, and it keeps saying "high-control." It never tells me whether my ordinary Baptist church counts. Because the phrase is never pinned down, it slides into meaning "conservative," and my child will read our congregation as a cult-lite.
**Suggested change:** In Chapter 1, after 1.2, add a short box: "**Is my church 'high-control'?** Not every conservative or evangelical church is. In this book, 'high-control' means specific behaviors: punishing questions, controlling who you can talk to or what you can read, making leaders unaccountable, or cutting off people who leave. Many churches with traditional beliefs do none of these. Many churches with progressive beliefs can do some of them. Judge by behavior, not by doctrine." Then check every later use, and where the point is really about evangelical teaching in general, say "some churches" instead.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 4. Chapter 1.3, "How It Usually Starts": snide examples
**Quote:** "why prayer seemed to work for the youth pastor's parking spots and not for your mother's cancer" / "a coworker from another religion who was obviously kinder than the elders"
**Reaction:** These lines are written to get a knowing laugh at believers' expense. "Obviously kinder than the elders" assumes the elders were unkind. The unanswered-prayer problem is real and serious, and putting it next to a parking-spot joke cheapens it.
**Suggested change:** "why prayer for your mother's cancer seemed to go unanswered" and "a coworker from another religion whose goodness didn't fit what you'd been told about outsiders."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 5. Chapter 1.3 and Appendix I: the "they hid it from you" frame
**Quote:** "Sometimes the thing that tipped it was learning a fact the church left out. (That's why this website is called what it's called.)"
**Reaction:** The brand name, *What They Skipped*, says my church concealed things on purpose. Some churches surely did skip things. But this frame tells my child that every hard fact is a revelation against faith, when serious Christian scholars have been working through these same facts for centuries.
**Suggested change:** Add a sentence after the parenthesis: "Leaving something out isn't always hiding it. Many of these facts are discussed openly by Christian scholars who remain believers, and some readers find that learning them deepens their faith rather than ending it."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 6. Chapter 4.2, "Four Facts That Help": all four point one way
**Quote:** "That blurred distinctions the original languages keep" / "Many people who believe in God don't believe in hell."
**Reaction:** I respect that fact 1 admits eternal punishment is the majority and official view, which is honest. But fact 2 hints that "hell" is mostly a translation artifact. It leaves out that the strongest word, *Gehenna*, appears mostly in the words of Jesus himself. Fact 4 uses a poll as reassurance one paragraph after telling me that feelings aren't evidence. Popularity isn't evidence either.
**Suggested change:** Add to fact 2: "The translation point doesn't remove the doctrine. Most of the New Testament's *Gehenna* sayings are attributed to Jesus, and every view of hell, from eternal punishment to universalism, has to account for them." Add to fact 4: "That number doesn't prove anything about hell. Polls don't settle doctrine. It shows only that this particular fear is not a requirement for believing in God."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 7. Chapter 6.2, "Three Piles": loaded examples in the "Return" pile
**Quote:** "rules about gender that never fit you, the instinct to report your own thoughts to an authority"
**Reaction:** "Rules about gender" are listed as something to throw out, which is a disputed moral question presented as an obvious example. "Report your own thoughts to an authority" makes confession and accountability partners, which many believers find freeing, sound like surveillance.
**Suggested change:** Move "rules about gender" to the "Unexamined" examples as "what you think about gender roles." Replace the second item with "the habit of letting someone else's approval decide whether a thought was allowed." Add one more "Keep" example that is plainly religious: "a practice of prayer or confession that actually helped you."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 8. Chapter 8: purity culture vs. the historic Christian sexual ethic
**Quote:** chapter subtitle "*You were taught to be afraid of the body you live in.*" / 8.3: "Some people react to purity culture by doing everything they were forbidden, all at once. Some people want to go slowly. Neither is the right way to heal."
**Reaction:** I'll grant most of the criticism of purity culture. The rings, the "used goods" talk, and the modesty blame hurt real people, and evangelicals have said so. But the chapter offers only two paths: indulge everything now, or indulge everything slowly. It never says you can drop the shame and still choose to wait for marriage because you believe it's right. Leaving that option out is a tilt.
**Suggested change:** Change the subtitle to "*Some of what you were taught made your body feel like the enemy.*" In 8.3, add a bullet: "**Rejecting the shame doesn't decide the ethic.** Some people leave purity culture behind and keep the traditional Christian view that sex belongs in marriage, now held for their own reasons instead of out of fear. Others change their view. Both are ways out of shame." In 8.1, add an evangelical critic of purity culture who keeps the traditional ethic, such as Rachel Joy Welcher, *Talking Back to Purity Culture* (IVP, 2020), and list her in Appendix E.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 9. Chapter 8.2–8.3: "sex-positive therapist" and vague "conservative critics"
**Quote:** "a sex-positive therapist" / "though some conservative critics dispute them."
**Reaction:** "Sex-positive" is an ideological label. To me it signals a therapist who might treat my child's convictions as the problem. "Some conservative critics" is the only place the book mentions conservatives disagreeing, and it names nobody, so I can't check it. The book says it wants me to check.
**Suggested change:** Replace with "a therapist trained in sexual health who respects your values, whatever they are." Either name and source the specific critiques of *The Great Sex Rescue* (for example, published reviews from complementarian outlets) or cut the clause and rely on the two methodological cautions, which are already good.
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 10. Chapter 9.2: the parents' "They say / They often mean" table
**Quote:** "| "Who has been influencing you?" | "I can't believe you got here on your own." |" / "| "We'll be praying for you." | Sometimes love. Sometimes a way to end the conversation. Often both. |"
**Reaction:** This is where I, the parent, am reading about myself. Chapter 9.1 is generous, and "fear wearing the costume of anger" is fair. The table, though, reads my mind and rarely lands on the charitable reading. When I say I'll pray for my child, I mean it as the most loving thing I know how to do.
**Suggested change:** Add a third column, "And sometimes," that gives the plain meaning: "Where did we go wrong?" → "I love you and want to understand." "Who has been influencing you?" → "I want to know what you've been reading so I can understand it too." "We'll be praying for you." → "I'm handing my worry to the one I trust most. It's the most loving thing I know to do." Add a line after the table: "Try asking which one they mean. Most parents will tell you."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 11. Chapter 10: treating real truth claims as a thinking distortion
**Quote:** "Many high-control churches teach in absolutes. Saved or lost. Heaven or hell. Truth or lies."
**Reaction:** "All-or-nothing thinking" is a real distortion when it's about people or yourself ("I'm a total failure"). But whether Jesus rose from the dead really does have two answers. Listing "truth or lies" next to cognitive distortions makes holding to Christian truth claims sound like a mental-health symptom, and I'd stop recommending the book right here.
**Suggested change:** Add to 10.2: "One distinction matters here. Some questions really do have two answers: either Jesus rose from the dead or he didn't. Holding a firm view on a factual or doctrinal question is not a distortion, whether you're a believer or an atheist. The distortion is applying that same either/or to *people and experiences*: a whole church, a whole family, or your whole past." Cut "Truth or lies." from the 10.1 list.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 12. Chapter 10.3: faith-stage models that put orthodoxy at the bottom
**Quote:** "Brian McLaren's *Faith After Doubt* (2021) describes four stages he calls simplicity, complexity, perplexity, and harmony."
**Reaction:** McLaren is a well-known progressive voice, and his model puts conventional believers in the earliest stage. The book says "No ranking" in Chapter 14, but this is a ranking. It's put gently, but it is one.
**Suggested change:** Add: "Stage models are contested. Critics, including many traditional Christians, point out that they tend to treat the author's own destination as the most mature, and that a person can hold traditional beliefs with plenty of depth and complexity. Use the map if it helps. Don't let it tell you which beliefs are 'more developed.'" Also identify McLaren in-line as "a leading voice in progressive Christianity," so readers know who is speaking.
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 13. Chapter 11.3: "At best, they gave them a vocabulary"
**Quote:** "The rules didn't create those convictions. At best, they gave them a vocabulary." (also 11.1: "almost none of them involve becoming a terrible person.")
**Reaction:** Where morality comes from is one of the most argued questions in philosophy, and here it's answered in two sentences against the religious view. The strongest theistic argument, that objective moral obligation needs a grounding, isn't mentioned. Neither is the historical argument that ideas like "every person has worth" came into the West largely through Christianity. That idea is in the book's own "keep" pile in Chapter 6.
**Suggested change:** Replace with: "Whether those convictions come ultimately from God is one of the oldest debates in philosophy. Many Christian thinkers argue that moral obligation only makes full sense if there is a God. Many non-religious philosophers disagree. Historians like Tom Holland (*Dominion*, 2019) argue that Western ideas about human dignity owe a great deal to Christianity. What's clear is that you don't lose your conscience when your beliefs change." Add Holland and one theistic ethicist (e.g., C. S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity*, Book 1) to Sources.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 14. Chapter 11.4 and workbook prompts: assuming the reader is leaving
**Quote:** Day 63: "Which rule from your old church do you now think was wrong?" / 14 Write #2: "Which one were you taught to fear most?" / Day 77: "Which one would your sixteen-year-old self be most shocked by?"
**Reaction:** The front matter welcomes the person "still going every Sunday," but the prompts assume someone on the way out who now rejects old rules. My child's questioning friend who ends up staying would find almost no prompts written for them.
**Suggested change:** Make the prompts two-sided. Day 63: "Pick one rule from your old church. Do you still hold it, have you changed it, or are you unsure? Why?" Add a Day 65 alternative: "Which of your church's convictions do you hold more strongly now than you did ninety days ago?" Day 77: "Which portrait would surprise your sixteen-year-old self, and which would they expect?"
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 15. Chapter 12.2: bundling "welcomes doubters" with "affirming"
**Quote:** "many denominations and individual churches now welcome doubters and LGBTQ+ people."
**Reaction:** This implies that only progressive churches welcome doubters, and that a traditional church can't be a good place to ask questions. Many people leave a harsh church for a healthier one that holds the same beliefs.
**Suggested change:** Split into two bullets. "**A different church with similar beliefs.** Some people find that the problem was one congregation's culture, not its creed, and land at a church that holds traditional beliefs but handles questions and doubt well." Then: "**Affirming or progressive congregations.** …" (keep the existing text, minus "doubters and").
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 16. Chapter 13: an uneven warning about counselors
**Quote:** "If you're leaving a faith, a counselor whose goal is to bring you back to it is not neutral help."
**Reaction:** True, and fair. But the book doesn't give the mirror-image warning. A therapist who treats a client's faith as the illness is also not neutral help, and the Appendix A resources point only toward secular providers.
**Suggested change:** Add: "The reverse is also true: if you decide to keep your faith, a therapist who treats your beliefs as the problem is not neutral help either." In 13.4 and Appendix A, note that licensed Christian therapists who don't impose faith are an option too (e.g., searchable via the Psychology Today directory's faith filter). Also soften "Scripture alone is sufficient for emotional problems" to the movement's own wording ("sufficient for counseling the problems of life"), sourced to ACBC.
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 17. Chapter 13.5: *Chiles v. Salazar*
**Quote:** "The ruling was about free speech. It did not find conversion therapy safe or effective."
**Reaction:** That's accurate, and I'm glad the book reports the ruling at all. But it gives only one side's reading of what the case means. The side that won argued it was about clients who want counseling that fits their religious goals, and the paragraph doesn't say so.
**Suggested change:** Add before the last two sentences: "Supporters of the ruling argued that bans prevented clients from receiving talk therapy aligned with their own religious goals."
**Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 18. Chapter 14.2: no portrait for staying or returning to orthodox faith
**Quote:** subtitle "*Short, fair portraits. No ranking.*" / "Some people find that what they left wasn't Christianity itself but one strict version of it."
**Reaction:** There are six portraits, and not one describes the person who comes out of deconstruction still holding to, or back in, historic evangelical faith, with deeper roots and fewer fears. "A different kind of Christianity" quietly assumes the person ends up somewhere other than where they started. For a parent, this is the destination I'm praying for, and the book acts as if it doesn't exist.
**Suggested change:** Add a portrait first or second in the list: "**Staying, or coming back.** Some people go through every question in this book and stay in evangelical or traditional Christianity, or return to it after time away. They often describe their faith afterward as quieter and more their own, held for reasons they've examined instead of fears they inherited. Some stay in their home church. Some move to a different congregation with similar beliefs. This isn't a failure to deconstruct. It's one honest place to land." Also consider citing Lifeway Research's 2019 study of young-adult church dropouts, which found that many who left for a year or more later returned (verify figures before print), in 14.1.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 19. Appendix D FAQ, "What if I go back?"
**Quote:** "Coming back on purpose, after looking hard at it, is different from never having looked."
**Reaction:** I'm glad this question is here, and the first two sentences are lovely. The last sentence, though, suggests that people like me who never left have never looked. Many lifelong believers have wrestled hard without walking out.
**Suggested change:** "Then you go back. People return to faith, sometimes the same faith and sometimes a different version. However you get there, a faith you've looked at honestly is your own."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 20. Appendix E, "Further Reading": no orthodox voices at all
**Quote:** The list: Klein, Boss, McLaren, Burns, Gregoire, Winell, and What They Skipped's own *Unbound* and *What They Actually Taught You*.
**Reaction:** This is the clearest proof that the book tilts. Every faith-related title is either by someone who left or by a progressive or reforming voice, plus the publisher's own guides. A reader who wants to see the best case for staying gets nothing. A book that says it doesn't care where you land should hand you maps to every destination.
**Suggested change:** Add a labeled subsection, "If you want the strongest case for staying," with three or four titles from serious orthodox writers who take doubt seriously, for example: Gavin Ortlund, *Why God Makes Sense in a World That Doesn't* (2021); Rebecca McLaughlin, *Confronting Christianity* (2019); Os Guinness, *God in the Dark: The Assurance of Faith Beyond a Shadow of Doubt* (1996); Rachel Joy Welcher, *Talking Back to Purity Culture* (2020). Add a parallel subsection, "If you've left or are leaving," for the existing titles, so the structure itself shows balance.
**Severity:** MUST FIX

### 21. Appendices E and I: "passages used against LGBTQ+ people"
**Quote:** "the seven passages used against LGBTQ+ Christians" / "*Unbound* for the passages used against LGBTQ+ people"
**Reaction:** "Used against" settles the interpretive question before you open the book. For most of church history, these passages have been read as stating a sexual ethic, not as weapons. The pointer to the 1946 RSV also implies the traditional reading is a recent mistranslation, which is the debate *Unbound* is making, not something already decided.
**Suggested change:** "the seven passages traditionally read as prohibiting same-sex relationships, and the scholarly debate over how to read them." In the front matter or Appendix I, add one transparent sentence: "What They Skipped's other guides argue for specific conclusions, including an LGBTQ+-affirming reading of Scripture. This workbook is meant to be useful whether or not you agree with them."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 22. Title page and source sections: "Every claim sourced"
**Quote:** "Every claim sourced." / Chapter 6 sources: "No outside claims."
**Reaction:** The factual citations are impressive. But many of the claims that shape how I see my own church aren't sourced at all: "High-control churches often teach faith as a package deal," the unattributed "If Genesis isn't literal, why trust any of it?", and what parents "often mean." Those come from the author's experience, and that's fine, but the book should say so.
**Suggested change:** Change the tagline to "Factual claims sourced." In Chapters 6, 9, and 10, label experience-based generalizations: "In the author's experience, and in many people's accounts, …" and change "No outside claims" to "The generalizations about churches and families in this chapter come from the author's experience and are not research findings."
**Severity:** SHOULD FIX

### 23. Chapter 7.3 rituals: only for endings
**Quote:** "The point is to mark the ending yourself instead of waiting for someone else to."
**Reaction:** This is a gentle and good chapter. The ritual list (burn a letter to God, attend a last service) only fits someone who has finished leaving, though. A person who is staying but has lost an old, fearful version of faith grieves too.
**Suggested change:** Add a bullet: "if you're still in faith, mark the end of the old, frightened version of it: rewrite a prayer you used to say out of fear in words you actually mean."
**Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

### 24. A short page for the people who love the reader
**Quote:** Front matter: "All three of those people are welcome here. So is the person who hasn't left anything yet…"
**Reaction:** Parents and spouses will pick this book up, because I did. A page addressed to us would do more to cool things down at our dinner table than anything else in the book.
**Suggested change:** Add an Appendix (or a half-page after "Before You Begin"): "**If someone you love gave you this book.** Your fear for them is real, and it comes from love. This book doesn't ask you to share their conclusions. It asks for something harder: stay close, listen more than you argue, and trust that a person who is still talking to you hasn't closed any doors. People who are given room to ask questions often end up closer to their families, whatever they decide." Add a short script for parents to Appendix C: "I don't understand all of this yet, but I'm not going anywhere."
**Severity:** NICE TO HAVE

## What works well (keep these)

### 25. KEEP: Chapter 2.2, honest caveats on "religious trauma"
**Quote:** "It is not a formal diagnosis." / "the study was funded, carried out, and published by the research center its authors belong to, and other researchers have questioned how its sample was drawn."
**Reaction:** This is where the book first earned my respect. It flags the funding of a study that supports its own audience, and it says plainly that "religious trauma" doesn't mean every religion is traumatic ("Some of your old friends had a very different experience in the same pews"). That's the opposite of propaganda.
**Suggested change:** None. Use this tone as the standard for Chapters 8, 10, and 11.
**Severity:** KEEP (no change)

### 26. KEEP: Chapter 4.2 fact 1 and the *Four Views on Hell* source
**Quote:** "Eternal conscious punishment has been the majority view for most of church history … Eternal punishment is still the official position of most large denominations"
**Reaction:** The book could have pretended the traditional view is fringe or new, and it doesn't. Citing a volume that includes Denny Burk's defense of eternal conscious torment shows the author read the other side.
**Suggested change:** None (see note 6 for a companion addition).
**Severity:** KEEP (no change)

### 27. KEEP: symmetry warnings in 9.4, 10.1, 12.2, 13.3, and 14.3
**Quote:** "Protect them from fear-based teaching, whichever direction it comes from." / "now they're sure, with the same intensity, that anyone who believes is a fool." / "Watch for spaces where anger at religion becomes the only topic" / "If I decide to stay religious, or decide not to be, will you support either direction?" / "any group, religious or not"
**Reaction:** These are the lines that make neutrality believable. They hold leavers to the same standard as churches. As a parent, I'm grateful my child is being warned against swapping one closed system for another.
**Suggested change:** None. If anything, do more of this; notes 2, 11, 15, and 16 apply the same symmetry where the book currently forgets to.
**Severity:** KEEP (no change)

### 28. KEEP: Front matter and Chapter 1.1, faith treated as a live option
**Quote:** "It is not anti-Christian. A lot of what follows was written with gratitude for the good things many people found in church: the music, the casseroles, the people who showed up at the hospital." / "Plenty of people deconstruct and remain Christian."
**Reaction:** These sentences tell me the author doesn't despise what I love. Together with the plain "where he's standing" author disclosure, they keep me reading after the rougher spots.
**Suggested change:** None. Make sure the rest of the book lives up to them (see notes 18 and 20).
**Severity:** KEEP (no change)

### 29. KEEP: Chapter 9.1 and the spouse section, fear reframed as love
**Quote:** "Often it's fear wearing the costume of anger." / "seeing a couples therapist who isn't trying to win either of you over"
**Reaction:** This is the most charitable thing anyone in this movement has said about people like me, and it's true. It gives my child a reason to stay at the table.
**Suggested change:** None.
**Severity:** KEEP (no change)
